Step B asks for ONE principled criterion that suppresses the uniform Sea to a tiny Λ while letting uniform matter/radiation drive normal expansion. The resolution is that c05 already supplies it: gravity is sourced by ∇(ΔSSV), the gradient relative to the local background, NOT by absolute SSV / absolute energy density. Take the Sea ground state as the reference (ΔSSV ≡ 0 for the unperturbed uniform Sea). Then:

- The uniform Sea, however Planck-scale its absolute energy density, has zero excess → sources zero gravity. The naive CC catastrophe (ρ_vac ~ ρ_P, ~120 orders too big) does not even arise, because CPP gravity never couples to absolute energy density. Crucially this is the SAME fact the DM picture already relies on ("uniform Sea locally inert"), now applied cosmologically — so it is not special pleading.
- Matter/radiation are localized EXCESSES above the ground state (ΔSSV > 0): they source gravity and, via the Step-A shell theorem, drive expansion. A smooth uniform matter density is still a uniform density of discrete excess concentrations, so M_enc(<r) > 0 — uniform matter gravitates, the ground state does not. This is exactly the Step-A consistency requirement, now met.
- Sea swirls = ΔSSV gradients → unsuppressed gravity = DM (requirement ii).
- Λ = the tiny RESIDUAL by which the real Sea departs from the perfect uniform reference. Not the full Sea energy; hence the suppression.

So matter, DM, and Λ all gravitate by ONE mechanism (gradient of ΔSSV-excess), differing only in what the gradient is. That is the principled distinction — requirement (iii) — from one mechanism, not three assumptions. The CC problem is restructured from "why 120 orders below ρ_P?" to "what is the residual non-uniformity of the Sea and why is it (l_P/R_H)²-scaled?" → Step C.

So "the Sea ground state is gravitationally inert" is a PREDICTION of gradient-sourcing that must be checked against the full GR limit: does CPP's T_μν correctly exclude ground-state vacuum energy while keeping the equivalence principle for excess energy?
