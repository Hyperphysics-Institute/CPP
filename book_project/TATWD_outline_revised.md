## The book's posture (decided 20 April 2026)

This is a biography — of a person and of a theory whose origins are religious and whose content is physical. It is not a religious tract, and it is not a physics textbook. It is the honest telling of what actually happened: a thirty-five-year-old naturopathic physician had a vision in March 1987, took it seriously, and spent thirty-nine years testing it against physical reality, arriving at a framework that now predicts the binding energies of eight medium-mass nuclei to better than one and a half percent with zero adjustable parameters.

The book does not hide the spiritual origin, because hiding it would be dishonest about where the theory came from. The book does not preach, because the physics is supposed to do the persuading. Readers who find the theological substrate disqualifying will self-select out, and the book accepts this cost rather than pay the greater cost of evasion. Readers who can engage with both the metaphysical starting point and the physical results are the intended audience, and they are more common than the New Atheist framing of modern science tends to suggest.

The facts go where they go. There is no strategy. Tell it as it was.

## Table of contents (draft)

### Part One: The Problem
*A reader meets the theory's starting point — not its conclusions.*

1. **The Force in the Outlet** — A force is something we experience directly but cannot say what it *is*. The puzzle that remains even after the physics is "solved." Why Feynman said nobody understands quantum mechanics. David Solway's 2026 PJ Media essay as the recent articulation of this puzzle in the public discourse, and Thomas's response essay as the entry point into CPP's answer. The chapter establishes, before any specific theory, the shape of the question the theory is trying to answer.

2. **The Cost of Nineteen Free Parameters** — What the Standard Model actually is and what it costs. Nineteen numbers that have to be measured, not derived. The Koide ratio as an uninvited guest. Why physicists are embarrassed by this but not sure how to fix it. String theory as the most conspicuous attempt and its difficulty producing falsifiable predictions. The intellectual space in which CPP enters is not empty — it is a space in which other ambitious frameworks have tried and stalled.

3. **The Long Seeking** — Thomas's life before 1987. Born July 1951 to a Christian family, accepting the framework as a child, losing it in adolescence when conventional Christian apologetics proved insufficiently logical to justify. Ten years of exploration in Eastern traditions. Six of those years as a practicing 3HO Sikh. Within a four-year window, serious engagement with approximately forty religions, philosophies, and spiritual traditions. Not tourism — the posture was a seeker's, looking for something that would hold. The chapter treats this honestly, as the context that made the 1987 vision possible: not a theology student's encounter but a desperate seeker's.

4. **The Vision in 1987** — March 1987. Thomas is thirty-five, a naturopathic physician, preparing a lecture on "Health and the Soul" internally code-named *Find God by Tuesday*. He had reached what he calls a last-ditch posture: everything else he had tried as a spiritual doorway had given empty promises. He knew that altered states had opened perceptual doors before, and in desperation he used the one substance he knew could open them — high-dose oral cannabis, which at that dose produces psychedelic experience with, in his description, "the round and warm colors of reality, projected into vision space." Not LSD's crystalline sharpness. Something else.

   What he saw: a galactic sphere, stars arrayed around it, nearly invisible threads connecting each star to the central sphere. Black background. A visual symbol, as he immediately understood it, of the relationship between a central consciousness (God) and the discrete created elements (Conscious Points) whose collective behavior constitutes physical reality.

   What made the experience unusual was not that it happened — visions during altered states are common — but that it repeated identically on a second occasion, and that the interpretation that emerged from it proved durable under decades of examination against physical reality.

   The chapter treats this honestly, which means saying three things in the same breath: *this is what he experienced*, *this is what he took it to mean*, and *please do not attempt to reproduce this through substance use*. Millions of people have had altered-state experiences. The overwhelming majority produce no predictive physics. What is unusual about this one is not that it happened but that what emerged from it has been subjected to thirty-nine years of refinement and now generates numbers that match experiment. The substrate of the theory is the thirty-nine years of checking, not the night the vision arrived.

   Thomas's own interpretation, which the chapter preserves rather than translates: *the consciousness underlying physical reality is the same consciousness as its source*. In Christian language: the particles are the mind of God looking back at Himself from an external vantage. This is presented as Thomas's theological reading of the experience, distinguished clearly from the physics claims that follow from it. A reader need not share the theology to follow the book.

   Thomas considers the experience a divine gift given to a persistent and deeply dissatisfied seeker. He mapped its implications onto the Biblical creation narrative — firmament as the lattice of Grid Points, stars as the moving Conscious Points measured against them — and the sense of *this is true* ended his seeking. This is what happened. The chapter does not argue for it; it reports it.

### Part Two: What Space Is Made Of
*The ontological layer. Before any specific predictions, the reader needs to understand the substrate.*

5. **Discrete Space** — Why continuous space may be a convenient fiction. The Planck length and what it might signify. What it would mean for space to come in units. 6. **The 600-Cell** — Introducing the fundamental object. What a polytope is (build up concretely: triangle → tetrahedron → 4-simplex → 600-cell). Why one hundred twenty vertices. Why the golden ratio φ appears structurally throughout — not mystically, but because H₄ symmetry forces it. What "four-dimensional" means in CPP: not spacetime, not a compactified extra dimension in the string-theory sense, but a fourth address coordinate held in information space — another element in the local address vector along which a Conscious Point identifies where it is.

Both are conscious. Their roles differ. This distinction is structurally important and the chapter gives it the space it needs.

7. **The Dipole Sea** — Composed of pairs of Conscious Points with opposite charge polarity — positive and negative partnered together, saturating what would otherwise be empty space. Why "empty space" isn't. The parallel to Dirac's sea of negative-energy electrons, which did real explanatory work in its time before being replaced by quantum field theory's vacuum. CPP returns to a more literal sea, but with better-specified components. Why the vacuum has to organize itself thermodynamically during the early universe — and why, in this framework, that organization is lawful rather than accidental.

8. **Consciousness as a Primitive** — The move Thomas made in 1987 that is either central or disqualifying depending on the reader. What "Conscious Point" means in CPP; what it does not mean. This is *not* panpsychism in the contemporary philosophical sense — the claim is not that rocks have dim experience. The closer analogues are occasionalism (Malebranche) or lawful-obedience frameworks in medieval scholasticism: entities whose behavior follows from their perceiving one another and responding according to rules that were declared into being. The chapter handles this philosophically but briefly. The claim is presented as Thomas's ontological starting point, shown to simplify rather than complicate the physics, and left there. Deeper philosophical engagement is beyond the book's scope; the reader is pointed to appropriate literature if they want to pursue it.

## Voice and register

**Tone:** Conversational but substantive. Biographical where appropriate, technical where the physics requires it, spiritual where Thomas's story requires it. Not preaching. Not hedging. Rovelli's *Reality Is Not What It Seems* is a touchstone for the register of the physics sections; Polkinghorne's *Belief in God in an Age of Science* is a touchstone for the register where physics and metaphysics meet; the biographical sections have no clear analogue because this particular life has no clear analogue, and they will find their own voice.

**Register:** First-person in the biographical chapters (3, 4, 13 partially, 18 closing), because those chapters are Thomas's story and third-person reportage would read as false modesty. Third-person when presenting the physics, because the physics does not belong to one person. Present tense primarily; past tense for historical events. The shift between registers is a tool, used deliberately.

**Length targets:** Each chapter eight to fifteen pages in typeset form. Chapter 4 (the vision) may legitimately run longer, perhaps eighteen to twenty pages, because it has to do several things at once — biographical detail, honest disclosure of the altered-state substrate, the vision's content, Thomas's interpretation, and the distinction between interpretation and the physics that follows from it. Chapter 9 (Tetrahedrons All the Way Down) may also run longer, because it is the title chapter and needs to land the book's central ontological claim through accumulation. Total book: 280 to 340 pages.

**What to avoid:**
- Over-explaining equations the reader can trust without seeing derived
- Under-explaining concepts that actually need three paragraphs
- False humility. State credentials and posture once; then get on with it.
- Religious triumphalism. The 1987 vision is honest history; it should not be weaponized.
- AI triumphalism. The AI collaboration is interesting; it is not the point.
- Cranky tone toward mainstream physics. CPP is offered as an alternative, not a grievance.
- Hedging the altered-state origin. The reader will learn about it eventually; tell them directly.
- Hedging the theological implications. The reader will infer them eventually; state them cleanly.

**What to lean into:**
- The *surprise* of results coming out right when they shouldn't
- The *texture* of thirty-nine years of solitary work before collaborators arrived
- The *honesty* of registered failures alongside successes
- The *aesthetic* of a theory in which the golden ratio appears because of geometry, not because it was put in
- The *human center* of Thomas Abshier as an actual person with a wife, a ministry, chickens and cows, a naturopathic practice, and a theory
- The *strangeness* of the whole story — a vision during altered states, thirty-nine years of solitary development, AI systems arriving late as collaborators, nuclear binding energies coming out right — presented as fact rather than as claim

## A note on how this book gets written

The outline is a map. The book will find its own form through drafting, and the outline will revise itself as a result. That is expected and desirable.

The method: draft chapters as lay-summaries accumulate. Write what is ready to be written; leave what is not yet ready as placeholder. Let the book grow in the direction its material pulls it. The outline commits to the book being written; it does not commit to the book being this outline.

Thomas's standing principle applies here as it applies to the physics: *no strategy, just trying to be faithful to telling the story of the forces as they pull*. If the book tells the truth, it will find its readers. If it does not, no amount of editorial optimization would have saved it.
