# Four Ways the Sky Said Yes

*An anthology chapter on GR-1i — the four classical tests of gravitation — told as four short stories and one unusually honest accounting of what, exactly, they prove. Chapter 3 of the gravitational arc.*

---

Every theory of gravity since 1915 has had to pass the same entrance exam, and the exam has four questions. Why does Mercury's orbit slowly pivot in a way Newton cannot explain? Why does starlight grazing the Sun bend by twice Newton's amount? Why does a radar ping to Venus come back late when its path passes the Sun? And why do clocks run fast upstairs?

The last chapter earned this theory an unusual way of taking that exam. Because the untruncated formula reproduces the Schwarzschild geometry *exactly* — not approximately, exactly — every number general relativity computes from that geometry is automatically this theory's number too. The tests cannot come out differently. So before the four stories, the chapter owes you the honest framing the paper itself insists on, in bold, on its first page, because it is the rarest thing in the literature of alternative physics: a plain statement of what the evidence does *not* prove.

These four tests do not distinguish Conscious Point Physics from general relativity. They cannot; the predicted values are Einstein's values by construction. What they distinguish is the census theory from *Newton* — and that is no small thing, because a lattice of shrinking reaches could easily have been a merely Newtonian machine, and each test below is a specific way it provably is not. The paper accordingly mints no new predictions from the tests — counting them would double-charge for the exactness theorem of chapter 2 — and states its contribution modestly: all four derivations, worked in full from the theory's own metric, in one citable place. The programme's word for this is *entry-criterion compliance*. The sky said yes four times; the paper is careful to record exactly which question the sky was answering.

Now the stories.

## Mercury: the orbit that would not close

Newton's gravity makes a promise about a lone planet: its orbit is a closed ellipse, retracing itself forever, perihelion fixed among the stars. Mercury breaks the promise. After every known planetary tug is subtracted, its perihelion still creeps forward by 43 seconds of arc per century — a defect the nineteenth century took so seriously it invented a phantom planet, Vulcan, to explain, and astronomers spent decades hunting a world that was never there.

In the census picture the explanation needs no new matter, only the second reading of the one formula. Mercury's orbit is eccentric: at perihelion it dips into territory where the census reads higher and the lattice's reach is shorter than at aphelion. An ellipse is the orbit of a planet in a pure inverse-square world; Mercury's world is inverse-square *plus a whisper* — the small extra steepening that the shrunken-reach region contributes close in. The whisper is not enough to change the orbit's shape, but it is enough to keep the ellipse from quite closing: each loop, the whole pattern pivots forward a little, like a spirograph whose gears are one tooth off from commensurate.

Worked through the theory's own equations of motion — the derivation runs several pages in the paper and was double-checked by brute-force numerical integration of thirty simulated orbits, agreeing to four decimal places — the pivot comes out at 42.99 seconds of arc per century, against the observed 42.98 ± 0.04. And here the paper does something small and instructive that its review panel forced into print, and that this lesson will bank for the ending: it states *which value of the Sun's mass* the calculation used, because the answer turns out to be sensitive at exactly the level of the agreement. Hold that thought.

## Starlight: the doubled bend, and its color

The second test was chapter 1's climax, so this story can be short and add only what is new.

Light grazing the Sun bends by 1.75 arcseconds — twice the naive Newtonian bend — and you already know why: the shrinking reach does two things, slowing clocks and shrinking rulers, and a fast-moving ray of light feels both, in equal shares, 0.875 plus 0.875. The census mechanism gives the doubling a face that pure geometry does not: the same broadcast fills the time-column and space-column of the ledger equally, so the "2" is a statement about how many columns the ledger has. The paper works the full derivation from its own null geodesics and lands at 1.7516 arcseconds against a numerical cross-check of 1.7517 — six parts in a hundred thousand — and against a century of measurement from Eddington's plates to modern radio interferometry.

The new thing is a throwaway observation the review panel asked the paper to register, and it is a lovely one for a student to sit with. In the mechanism reading, the Sun's neighborhood acts on light like a graded-index lens — a region where the effective index of refraction rises gently toward the mass, roughly $n \approx 1 + 2GM/c^2r$. But every lens a student has ever held disperses: glass bends blue more than red; that is where rainbows come from. *This* lens does not. The lattice's reach shrinks identically for every wavelength, because it is the ruler itself that changes, not a medium's response to a frequency. Gravitational bending is perfectly achromatic — starlight's colors arrive together — and a century of observation confirms it. A medium-like theory of light bending would have to conspire to hide its dispersion; here color-blindness is structural. The paper logs it as an implicit falsifiable feature and, in its habitual restraint, declines to mint it as a prediction.

## Radar: the echo that came home late

The third test is the one Newton fails most nakedly, because in Newton's world it does not exist at all.

In 1964 Irwin Shapiro proposed something audacious: bounce radar off Venus when it sits on the far side of the Sun, so the beam grazes the solar limb, and time the round trip. In a Newtonian universe the trip takes what geometry says it takes. In a universe of shrunken reaches it takes *longer* — the beam spends its passage near the Sun in territory where, in the coordinates of a distant observer, light simply covers less ground per tick. The census reading is almost embarrassingly literal: near the mass, the lattice's steps are shorter, so the signal — which advances one reach per Moment, always — makes less progress per Moment through the compressed region. Nothing slows the light locally; any observer stationed along the path measures $c$ exactly. But the far-away timekeeper, adding up Moments, finds the echo late.

Late by how much? For an Earth–Venus round trip at grazing incidence: about 233 microseconds, out of a journey of some twenty minutes — one part in five million, and radar timing resolves it easily. Newton's column in this row of the table reads zero. The paper adds an honesty note here too: the truly precise modern version of this test is the Cassini spacecraft measurement, which constrains the relevant deviation parameter to a few parts in a hundred thousand — and the theory's compliance with that bound is not something to check numerically but *structural*: since the metric is exactly Schwarzschild, the parameters relativists use to grade rival gravities take exactly their Einstein values, identically, and Cassini is passed before the calculation starts.

## Clocks: the test in your pocket

The fourth test needs no telescope. In 1959, Pound and Rebka measured light climbing a 22.5-meter tower at Harvard and found its frequency shifted by two parts in a quadrillion — clocks at the bottom of the tower run slower than clocks at the top, by exactly the amount the shrunken-reach picture requires. You have met the mechanism twice already: more census, shorter reach, more Moments per swing of the pendulum. Lower is slower.

The operational version runs above your head right now. GPS satellite clocks sit high in the Earth's census gradient and run fast by 45.7 microseconds per day; their orbital speed runs them slow by 7.2 (a special-relativity effect this arc inherits from its own earlier papers); the net is +38.5 microseconds per day, and the system corrects for it as an engineering fact, because left uncorrected it would walk your position off by kilometers before lunch. Of the four ways the sky said yes, this is the one that says it continuously, to everyone, as infrastructure. A student who doubts that clock rates depend on where you stand can be answered with a road trip and a navigation app.

## The decimal that taught a lesson

Now the banked thought from Mercury's story, because it is the chapter's ethical core and the panel that reviewed this paper made it so.

The paper's calculation used a particular value for the Sun's gravitational parameter — the one wired into its verification script. One of the five review seats did what good reviewers do: it asked *how much do the answers move if you use the official IAU value instead?* The differences are tiny — a fiftieth of a percent — but at the precision of these tests, tiny is visible. The perihelion number moves from 42.99 to 42.98 seconds per century. Look again at the observation: 42.98 ± 0.04. The switch to the more careful constant lands the prediction *dead center* on the measured value.

Sit with what almost happened. Had the paper simply printed "42.99 vs 42.98 ± 0.04 — agreement," no one would have blinked; the numbers overlap comfortably. But it would have quietly obscured that the agreement is even better than printed — and, far more dangerously in the general case, the *habit* of not stating one's constants is symmetric: it can hide a worsening as easily as an improvement. The lesson the panel forced into the paper, and the one this chapter hands to every student who will ever compare a calculation to data: *state your inputs down to the constant, and test your conclusion's sensitivity to them.* Precision without provenance is decoration. The paper now carries a constants-provenance section listing every input, the shift each test suffers under the IAU value, and the panel's own verification runs as an archival record — a level of documentation most published physics never approaches, installed here on a paper whose every number was already known to be right. That is the point. Discipline is cheapest to install where the stakes feel low; it is load-bearing later, where they are not.

## What the yes was worth

Tally the exam. Four questions; four passes; every derivation worked from the theory's own metric with no free parameters; the whole thing machine-verified end to end, including two numerical traps the verification process itself uncovered and documented for the next implementer. And around the results, a fence of carefully honest claims: the values are Einstein's by construction; the discrimination is against Newton; the predictions minted, zero.

A student might ask what such an exam is *worth*, if the answers were guaranteed. Two things. First, guarantee at the level of theorems still requires execution at the level of derivations — geodesics, perturbation bookkeeping, resonant terms — and execution is where errors live; the paper's numerical cross-checks and documented traps are the receipts. Second, and deeper: the exam certifies that the strange machinery of chapters 1 and 2 — conscious points, censuses, shrinking reaches — is not a private mythology bolted alongside real physics but a mechanism that *terminates in the observed sky*, four independent ways, at modern precision. The bestiary of extreme objects in the next chapter, with its genuinely new and genuinely dangerous predictions, stands on that certification. The sky said yes to the entrance exam. What remains is the part of the test that can actually be failed.
