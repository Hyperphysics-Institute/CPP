# The Highest Note

*The story of TP-1: how a single photon can be cut in half, why the cutting seems to make infinitely many photons out of nothing, and how that infinity turns out to be the sound of someone asking a lattice to play a note higher than it has strings for.*

---

## A photon, and a pair of scissors

Begin with the simplest object in quantum optics: one photon. A single quantum of light, travelling along, say, an optical fibre. It has a definite energy, a definite direction, a wavefunction spread out along its path like a long thin pulse. There is exactly one of it. If you put a perfect photon counter at the far end, it clicks once.

Now do something that sounds harmless. Halfway along the pulse, close a shutter — fast. Not a leisurely mechanical blink, but as close to instantaneous as you can manage. You have, in the most literal sense, cut the photon. The front half is past the shutter and keeps going; the back half is stopped.

What is on each side of the shutter now?

The naive answer is "half a photon on each side," but quantum mechanics does not sell half-photons. The honest answer, worked out in careful detail by Isak Cecil Onsager Rukan, Jan Gulla, and Johannes Skaar in a 2026 paper accepted to *Physical Review Letters* with the deadpan title *A truncated photon*, is stranger and more interesting. On each side of the shutter, taken by itself, you find a perfectly sensible state: a single photon on one side, vacuum — nothing — on the other. Look locally and everything is tidy. But the two sides together are not in a definite state at all. They are entangled, and worse, the global state is a superposition over *every possible photon number*: a little bit of zero photons, a little bit of one, a little bit of two, three, and on up without obvious end. Cutting one photon has produced a state that, considered as a whole, contains a smear of zero-to-infinitely-many photons.

Where did the extra photons come from? Nowhere, and that is the point. They were made by the cut itself. A shutter that slams shut is a boundary condition that changes in time, and in quantum field theory a boundary that changes in time does work on the vacuum. The mathematical name for the bookkeeping is *broken time-translation invariance*: the laws governing the photon were the same yesterday and today, but the shutter's slam picks out a particular instant, breaks that symmetry, and the energy that symmetry would have protected is free to come out as real particles. It is the same physics, in miniature, as the dynamical Casimir effect — the production of photons from the vacuum by a rapidly moving mirror — which was actually observed in a superconducting circuit in 2011. Rukan, Gulla and Skaar simply pushed the idea to its sharpest form: not a wobbling mirror, but a single photon and an idealized instantaneous cut.

## The infinity in the scissors

Here is the part that should make you uneasy, and that the rest of this story is about.

Rukan, Gulla and Skaar can calculate the *expected number* of photons the cut produces — the average click-count you would measure over many repetitions. And in the idealized limit, the limit where the shutter closes infinitely fast, that expected number is infinite.

Not large. Infinite.

The reason is clean enough to state. A truly instantaneous cut is, mathematically, a step function — a sharp edge in time. Sharp edges in time are made, by Fourier's theorem, out of waves of every frequency, and the very high frequencies fall off only slowly, as one over the frequency. When you add up the energy these frequencies carry into real photons, you are summing one-over-frequency across all frequencies, and that sum — the harmonic series, in effect — diverges. Logarithmically, slowly, but it diverges. The faster you cut, the more high-frequency content the cut contains, and the photon count climbs without bound.

Physicists have a standard, slightly embarrassed way of handling this. You declare that the sum does not really run all the way to infinite frequency; you put a ceiling on it — a *cutoff* — and you say the cutoff can be "arbitrarily high." Rukan, Gulla and Skaar do exactly this, and they are completely right to: in ordinary quantum field theory there is no fundamental highest frequency, so the cutoff is a formal device, a placeholder, a way of saying "we know this integral really stops somewhere, we just don't have a theory of where." The divergence is parked rather than cured. For any *real* shutter, which closes in some finite time rather than instantaneously, the number comes out finite and usually tiny; the infinity lives only in the idealized limit. But it is an infinity that the standard framework cannot say anything physical about. It is a placeholder waiting for a theory of the highest frequency.

This is exactly the kind of gap into which Conscious Point Physics has something to say — and exactly the kind of place where it is worth being honest about how *much* it has to say.

## What CPP is, in one breath

Conscious Point Physics is a programme that tries to build the Standard Model of particle physics out of a single geometric object: a four-dimensional polytope called the 600-cell, a crystal of 120 points in which each point has exactly twelve nearest neighbours. The number twelve — the *coordination number* of the lattice, written $z$ — recurs throughout the theory like a structural signature. The familiar particles and forces are supposed to be patterns of excitation on this lattice; light, in particular, is a ripple propagating through a medium of paired points called the Dipole-Pair Sea, the CPP version of the vacuum.

For most of this story you do not need the machinery. You need just one consequence of it, and it is a consequence that any crystal shares.

A crystal cannot carry waves of arbitrarily short wavelength. Think of a guitar string, but made of discrete beads on a thread rather than a continuous line. You can set up a wave that wiggles the beads gently, a long slow wave; you can set up shorter and shorter waves; but there is a shortest one — the one where each bead swings opposite to its neighbours, and there is no room for anything finer, because there is nothing between one bead and the next to wiggle. A shortest wavelength means a *highest frequency*. The bead-string has a top note, and above it, silence — not quiet, but literally no modes, no possible vibrations at all.

A real crystal — and the 600-cell lattice is, for this purpose, a crystal — has exactly this property. There is a highest frequency it can carry, set by the spacing of its points and the stiffness of the coupling between them. Above that frequency the lattice has no vibrations to offer. Not suppressed vibrations; *absent* ones.

## Where the infinity goes

Now put the two halves together, and watch the infinity disappear.

The truncated-photon divergence was a sum of one-over-frequency carried out to infinite frequency. It diverged because, in the continuum theory, there is no highest frequency — the integral has no top. But a photon in CPP is not a continuum object; it is a ripple on the 600-cell lattice, and the lattice has a top note. The sum does not run to infinity because there are no modes out there to sum over. It runs up to the lattice's highest frequency and then simply stops, because that is where the spectrum ends.

The infinity was never a fact about the world. It was a fact about pretending the medium could carry arbitrarily high frequencies. A real medium cannot, and the moment you grant the photon a real medium, the integral acquires a top and the answer becomes finite. This is the one genuinely CPP-specific thing the paper says: the regularization that ordinary field theory puts in by hand, as a formal cutoff "arbitrarily high," is in CPP not a hand at all but the edge of the lattice's own spectrum.

And here is the detail that turns a generic observation into a fingerprint. The lattice's highest frequency is not some anonymous Planck-scale number. It is fixed by the geometry of the 600-cell, and specifically by the largest of the handful of numbers — the *eigenvalues* — that characterize how a vibration spreads from each point to its neighbours. For the 600-cell that largest eigenvalue is exactly twelve: the coordination number, the count of each point's nearest neighbours. The highest frequency the lattice can carry works out to the square root of twelve, divided by the fundamental tick of time in the theory (the Planck time). The ceiling on the photon count therefore carries the number twelve inside it — the same twelve that counts the neighbours of every point in the crystal, the same twelve that runs through the rest of CPP. A theory that merely said "put the cutoff at the Planck scale" would have no reason to involve twelve. CPP's ceiling does, because in CPP the ceiling is not an arbitrary scale but the literal top of a specific lattice's spectrum.

When you put the numbers in, the once-infinite photon count becomes a finite number of order a few tens — the coefficient times about sixty-four and a half, for an optical photon. An infinity has become a definite, if academic, number, and the reason it became finite is that the medium has a highest note, stamped with a twelve.

## The discipline of not overclaiming

It would be easy to tell this story as a triumph, and it would be a mistake. The paper, TP-1, is careful about its own modesty, and the carefulness is the most characteristic thing about it — and about the programme that produced it.

Start with what TP-1 does *not* do. It does not predict the truncated photon. Rukan, Gulla and Skaar derived that effect inside ordinary quantum optics, and it is correct whether or not CPP is. TP-1 does not improve on their derivation, does not find an error in it, does not claim the effect as its own. What it does is show that the effect *fits* — that every piece of structure the standard derivation assumes has a home in CPP. The cuttable photon is a ripple in the Dipole-Pair Sea. The spray of photon numbers is the lattice's bosonic modes piling up. The tidy-locally-but-entangled-globally structure is CPP's account of measurement, in which looking at one side means tracing over the other. The shutter doing work on the vacuum is the dynamical Casimir effect read on a moving boundary of the Sea. None of these is a new mechanism; each is a standing piece of CPP doing exactly what it already does. The right word, which the paper uses on itself, is *embedding*: CPP provides a substrate in which the known effect lives, not a new explanation of why it happens.

And even the one genuinely new thing — the finite ceiling from the lattice's top note — comes with an honest asterisk. The paper pins down the *shape* of the answer: that the divergence is logarithmic, that it is cured by the band top, that the band top carries the twelve. What it does not yet pin down is the exact value of the leading coefficient, the order-one number multiplying the logarithm. Getting that number requires a harder calculation — summing a particular quantity over all the lattice's modes, near the top of the band where the modes crowd together — and that calculation is left explicitly open, registered in the programme's ledger of unfinished business under the name OPEN-TP-1. Two of the four independent reviewers who read the paper before it shipped said precisely this, in precisely these words: until that mode-sum is done, the framework-specific content is real but modest — a known divergence, plus a physically-grounded ceiling, with the height of the ceiling's coefficient still to be computed. The paper agrees with them in its own text. It ships as version 1.0 not because it is finished but because what it claims is true and clearly bounded, with the unfinished part named rather than hidden.

This is the texture of the whole CPP programme, and TP-1 is a small clean specimen of it. A result is allowed to ship when it is honest about its edges. A reviewer's objection, if it is right, is folded in rather than fought. An infinity is allowed to be cured only to the extent the theory can actually cure it, and the leftover — here, a single order-one coefficient — is written into the open-problems list in plain sight. The drama, such as it is, is not a triumphant prediction. It is the quieter satisfaction of watching an infinity that the standard theory could only park turn out, in a theory with a real and finite medium, to have been the sound of someone asking the lattice for a note higher than it has strings for. There is no such note. The lattice's highest string vibrates at the square root of twelve over the Planck time, and above it there is nothing — and that nothing is exactly enough to make the answer finite.

## What it opens

TP-1 is the first paper in a new corner of the programme: quantum optics, the physics of photons and detectors and beam splitters. It is deliberately positioned as a bridge rather than a destination — the place where the standard vocabulary of quantum optics, the Fock states and mode decompositions and time-dependent boundaries, is first translated into the language of the Dipole-Pair Sea. The truncated photon is a good first case precisely because it is dramatic and clean and already settled by someone else: it lets the translation be checked against a known answer before the harder optical phenomena — beam splitters, entanglement swapping, the homodyne measurements at the heart of quantum-optical experiments — are attempted on the same lattice.

And it leaves behind one sharp, finite question, the kind the programme likes best: compute that order-one coefficient from the crowding of modes at the top of the band, and the ceiling on the cut photon's count becomes not just finite but exactly known — a definite number, derived from the geometry of a four-dimensional crystal, for the average number of photons you make when you cut one photon as fast as the universe allows. The infinity is already gone. What remains is to measure the exact height of the ceiling that replaced it.
