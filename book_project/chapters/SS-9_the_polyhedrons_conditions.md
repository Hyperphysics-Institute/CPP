# The Polyhedron's Conditions

*The story of SS-9: how the simplicial-polytope structure that SS-7 had to assume turned out to be derivable from the framework's primitives — conditional on four named physical conditions that are not, themselves, yet derived; how a theorem from 1922 and a classification from 1947 did most of the work; and how the proof's honest scoping became the model for what an unfinished theorem in physics looks like when it ships.*

---

## What Was Assumed

SS-7's chapter ended with two questions left open. SS-8 took up the second one — what the extra-neutron physics signal turned out to mean — and answered it. The first question stayed open, and went into the registry as the highest-leverage problem in the strong-sector series.

The first question had this shape. SS-7's binding-energy formula required that the alpha clusters, in bound nuclei of moderate size, be arranged as the vertices of a *simplicial 3-polytope* — a convex polyhedron whose every face is a triangle. The $E = 3V - 6$ identity that gave the formula its zero-parameter character was a theorem about simplicial polytopes specifically, not about arbitrary clusters. If you wanted to count the alpha-alpha contact edges by Euler's formula, you needed every face to be a triangle. Otherwise the count was off.

SS-7's empirical agreement was striking. Twelve nuclei from carbon-12 to nickel-56, all within 1.5%, no parameters tuned. But the agreement rested on the simplicial-polytope arrangement being the actual one. The framework's primitives — the conscious points, the dipole pair sea, the 600-cell tetrahedral substrate, the K₃ collective-mode quantum — did not, on their face, *force* alpha clusters to arrange themselves as simplicial polytopes. They permitted it. They made it geometrically natural in some sense that was difficult to make precise. But they did not produce a derivation that ended with the words *and therefore the cluster is a simplicial 3-polytope*.

The gap was registered as OPEN-SS-24 at the close of SS-7 v1.2, in April 2026. The label given to the assumption itself was C4 — the fourth in a sequence of structural conditions the formula required and the framework had not yet produced. SS-7 had been honest about this from the start. The paper said, in §2.4, that C4 was a structural hypothesis pending first-principles closure. SS-8 inherited C4 unchanged, used it without alteration, and shipped its own conditional theorem on top of it. The structure it was building was as solid as C4 was, no more.

This is the situation SS-9 stepped into.

## The Condition Inheritance

The mainstream alpha-cluster picture has been worked since the 1930s. Lothar Wheeler observed early that alpha substructures persist inside larger nuclei. David Brink, in the 1960s, gave the picture quantitative form with what became the Brink-Bloch wavefunctions — explicit antisymmetrized many-body wavefunctions for alpha clusters in specified geometric arrangements. The Antisymmetrized Molecular Dynamics (AMD) program of Yoshiko Kanada-En'yo and others, developed since the 1990s, produces equilibrium cluster configurations by minimizing energy from a chosen ansatz. These approaches *find* polytopal arrangements empirically — the ground states of carbon-12 do come out triangular, oxygen-16 tetrahedral, neon-20 trigonal-bipyramidal — but they do not, in any standard form, *derive* the polytopal arrangement from a more fundamental structural picture. The polytope is the result of a calculation that takes the alpha-particle picture as given.

CPP's situation in early 2026 was different in a specific way. The framework had, in its toolbox, the K₃ collective-mode quantum from SS-5, the alpha binding constant from SS-5, and the 600-cell substrate that organizes everything from quark masses upward. It also had a particular picture of what alphas *are*: tetrahedral cages of nucleons, themselves built from quark-cage substructures, sitting on the 600-cell lattice. When two alphas meet, they meet face-to-face — the K₃ collective-mode binding $B_\text{pair}$ that operates inside an alpha at the nucleon-nucleon scale recurs at the alpha-alpha scale, unrescaled, as the same quantum operating between contact-shared K₃ faces. This recurrence is one of the things SS-7 and SS-8 both used, and it gave the framework a specific picture of what *kind* of contact between alphas was producing the binding.

The picture had a corollary. If alphas, when they touch, touch face-to-face — and the contact face is a K₃, three nucleons in a triangle — then the alphas in a bound cluster present specific outer faces to their neighbors. The original strict version of this condition, which SS-7 called C1 in its early drafts, said that each alpha presents *four* outer faces (the four faces of the alpha tetrahedron). This worked at small $N_\alpha$. It broke at $N_\alpha \geq 7$. The Freudenthal–van der Waerden classification of convex deltahedra, which we will get to in a moment, includes shapes whose vertices have *five* incident edges — the pentagonal bipyramid at $N_\alpha = 7$, the snub disphenoid at $N_\alpha = 8$, the icosahedron at $N_\alpha = 12$, all the way up to vertices with degree five. An alpha at such a vertex needs to present five contact faces, not four. The strict-C1 picture could not host this.

The refinement to C1$'$ came in SS-7 v1.3. Each alpha presents *some number* of outer faces, depending on its multi-facet count and the geometry it sits in. Refined-C1 is more permissive than strict-C1, and the cost is that the framework has to do more bookkeeping about which faces are presented in which configurations. But it is the right condition for the picture, because it is the condition that survives the FvdW arithmetic. SS-9 inherited refined-C1, refined-C2 (face-to-face contact at a specific distance $R_{\alpha\alpha}$), and C3 (K₃ collective mode at each contact). These three conditions came from SS-7 unchanged.

The question SS-9 asked was: starting from refined-C1, C2, and C3, what additional conditions are needed to derive that the alpha-cluster contact graph is the 1-skeleton of a simplicial 3-polytope?

## The Three Lemmas

The path through the work has the structure of three lemmas. None of the three is novel mathematics. What is novel is the choice of which lemmas to chain together, and the identification of which conditions each one requires.

**Lemma A** says: under refined-C1 and C2, every pair of alphas in contact is connected by a triangular K₃ face. The lemma does not say which alphas are in contact. It says that *if* two alphas touch, they touch this way. The output of Lemma A is therefore a *graph* — the *contact graph* of the cluster, with one vertex per alpha and one edge per K₃-face contact — but Lemma A by itself does not determine the graph's shape. The graph could be sparse (few contacts), dense (many contacts), or anywhere in between. Some additional condition is needed to fix which graph the ground state realizes.

**Lemma C** supplies that condition. Under C3 (each K₃ contact stores $B_\text{pair}$ binding) and C5 (the cluster realizes the ground state of total binding), the ground-state contact graph is the one with the *maximum* number of edges geometrically realizable. Energy minimization picks the densest graph the geometry allows. The lemma is essentially a variational statement: among all graphs that could realize a given $N_\alpha$ vertex set under the rigid-packing constraints, the ground state picks the one that has the most edges. C5 is the condition that the ground state actually exists and is the configuration realized — and this turns out to require its own sub-lemma, which we will come back to.

**Lemma B$'$** is the bridge from graph to polytope. Combining Lemma A (every pair of alphas in contact shares a triangular face) and Lemma C (the cluster realizes the maximum-edge graph) with three further conditions — C6 (the cluster's outer surface realizes a closed two-manifold), C7 (the contact graph is planar), and C8 (the abstract polytope identified by the contact graph admits a geometric realization at uniform edge length) — Lemma B$'$ concludes that the contact graph is the 1-skeleton of a convex 3-polytope. The mechanism by which Lemma B$'$ closes is *Steinitz's theorem*, and it is the keystone of the proof.

Ernst Steinitz proved, in 1922, a theorem that has since become one of the most-loved results in convex geometry. A graph $G$ is the 1-skeleton — the vertex-and-edge graph — of some convex 3-polytope if and only if $G$ is *3-vertex-connected* (no two vertices, when removed, disconnect the graph) and *planar* (the graph can be drawn in the plane without edge crossings). The theorem is purely combinatorial. It does not depend on which embedding of the polytope you choose, or on the lengths of any edges. It is a statement about graphs alone.

Steinitz's theorem is exactly what Lemma B$'$ needs. The contact graph of a maximum-edge alpha cluster is a graph; if it is 3-vertex-connected and planar, then by Steinitz it *is* the 1-skeleton of a convex 3-polytope. Whether the planarity condition holds is exactly what C7 supplies. Whether the 3-vertex-connectivity holds follows from the maximum-edge selection in Lemma C combined with a non-degeneracy argument that we will return to. Steinitz then closes the bridge: the contact graph is identified with a polytope's edge structure.

But the lemma needs one more thing. Steinitz's theorem gives you the *abstract* polytope — the combinatorial structure, the list of vertices and edges and faces. It does not give you a *metric* polytope — a specific geometric realization with concrete edge lengths and vertex positions. For SS-7's binding formula, the metric matters. The contact distance $R_{\alpha\alpha}$ is a specific number; the formula assumes that all the edges of the polytope realize this same distance. The claim that the abstract polytope from Steinitz admits such a uniform-edge-length realization is the content of C8.

C8 is the condition that turned out to be most subtle, and that I had not noticed in the first draft.

## What ChatGPT Caught

In late April 2026, in the first AI review pass on SS-9 v0.4, ChatGPT pointed out a gap in the proof that I had glossed over. Lemma B$'$ in v0.4 invoked Steinitz's theorem to identify the contact graph with a convex 3-polytope, then directly substituted the polytope's edge count into the binding formula. The substitution required that the polytope's edges all have length $R_{\alpha\alpha}$. But Steinitz's theorem alone does not give you that. Steinitz tells you the polytope *exists* as an abstract combinatorial object; it does not tell you that the specific embedding realized by the alpha-cluster centroids has uniform edge lengths.

The Freudenthal–van der Waerden classification, from 1947, supplies the answer for the specific shapes the alpha cluster can form. Hans Freudenthal and Bartel Leendert van der Waerden classified the convex deltahedra — the convex 3-polytopes whose every face is an equilateral triangle. There are exactly eight of them. The tetrahedron ($N_\alpha = 4$). The triangular bipyramid ($N_\alpha = 5$). The octahedron ($N_\alpha = 6$). The pentagonal bipyramid ($N_\alpha = 7$). The snub disphenoid ($N_\alpha = 8$). The triaugmented triangular prism ($N_\alpha = 9$). The gyroelongated square bipyramid ($N_\alpha = 10$). The icosahedron ($N_\alpha = 12$). At those eight values of $N_\alpha$, and only at those, a convex polyhedron exists whose every face is an equilateral triangle. At $N_\alpha = 11$, $13$, and $14$, no such polyhedron exists. (The non-convex deltahedra do extend the list, but the FvdW classification is specifically for convex realizations.)

C8 is the claim that the alpha-cluster centroids actually realize the FvdW deltahedron at each $N_\alpha$ where one exists — that the abstract polytope identified by Steinitz is, at FvdW values, the FvdW deltahedron, embedded with its edges at uniform $R_{\alpha\alpha}$. The classification supplies the candidate shape; C8 supplies the claim that the alpha cluster realizes it.

ChatGPT's review surfaced the implicit Steinitz-to-centroid realization step as a real gap, not a stylistic quibble. The fix was structural: register C8 as a paper-level structural hypothesis, register the corresponding first-principles-closure question as OPEN-SS-37, and rework Theorem clause (iv) into a two-part structure — abstract identification via Steinitz, geometric realization via C8. The hypothesis stack went from C1$'$ + C2 + C3 + C5 + C6 + C7 to C1$'$ + C2 + C3 + C5 + C6 + C7 + C8. The proof became more honest. This was the main effect of the d.1 review pass: an implicit assumption made explicit, with the corresponding open problem registered for follow-up rather than papered over.

## The Sub-Lemmas

The proof has three sub-lemmas, each addressing one of the conditions the main lemmas invoke. The sub-lemmas are where most of the actual work sits.

**Sub-Lemma 2.1** addresses C7 — the planarity of the contact graph. The natural worry is that nothing in C1$'$ + C2 + C3 + C5 + C6 obviously forces the contact graph to be planar. A graph drawn from physical contact between three-dimensional objects is, in principle, free to be non-planar. The sub-lemma shows that under refined-C1, C2, C6, and two further auxiliary conditions — H4 (cluster contractibility) and H5 (alpha-surface adjacency) — the contact graph is forced to be planar. The proof has four steps. The cluster's outer surface $\Sigma$ is shown to be homeomorphic to a 2-sphere (via boundary-Euler), the surface is decomposed into externally-presented faces, the alpha-dual graph embeds into the surface, and a generic non-crossing argument completes the planarity claim.

The sub-lemma's status is *conditional closure modulo* H4 and H5. Both auxiliary conditions are tractable: H4 follows from C5 isoperimetric arguments (handle and void configurations are excluded under ground-state energy minimization), and H5 from C5 plus enumerative arguments about edge-shared multi-alpha configurations. Neither has been fully closed yet. OPEN-SS-33 was advanced from "raw open" to "conditionally closed modulo H4 + H5" via Sub-Lemma 2.1 — a partial closure, not a full one.

**Sub-Lemma 2.2** is the 3D-non-degeneracy claim. Under C1$'$ + C2 + C3 + C5 at FvdW values of $N_\alpha$, the ground-state cluster cannot have all its centroids coplanar. The proof has four steps that exclude the degenerate planar case: a face-normal argument shows that coplanar configurations have at most degree-2 vertices, planar maximum-edge graphs satisfy $|E| \leq N_\alpha$, three-dimensional simplicial 3-polytopes satisfy $|E| = 3N_\alpha - 6$, and the strict edge gain at three dimensions forces the ground-state cluster to be three-dimensional. Sub-Lemma 2.2 closes a gap that had been listed as the third entry in §9 of v0.1.

**Sub-Lemma 2.3** is the well-definedness of C5. The condition C5 — that the ground state of the cluster exists and is realized — is not automatically satisfied; it requires that the supremum of binding over the configuration space is attained, not just approached. Sub-Lemma 2.3 establishes that the supremum is attained at $N_\alpha \geq 2$, via a five-step argument: $G$-connectedness gives a diameter bound, the configuration space is pre-compact under $\mathrm{SE}(3)$ quotient, the binding functional is upper-semicontinuous via closed face-coincidence subvarieties, attainment follows from a maximizing sequence and upper-semicontinuity, and a linear-chain floor places the maximizer interior to the configuration space. Sub-Lemma 2.3 partially closes a gap from §9 of v0.1.

The three sub-lemmas are the proof's actual content. They are technical, they are honest about which conditions they invoke, and they leave the corresponding programme-level open problems registered explicitly. None of the three closes a condition unconditionally; each replaces one black-box condition with a smaller, named gap that is more tractable to attack.

## What Grok Suggested

The eight-panel figure that the v0.8 paper carries was Grok's idea.

Grok's d.5 review in early May 2026 — the fifth AI review pass on SS-9, after ChatGPT's d.1, Copilot's d.2, ChatGPT's d.3, and the cache-resolution diagnostic d.4 — included a specific pedagogical suggestion. The proof references the FvdW classification in several places and walks through the cases at FvdW values, but the paper had no figure showing what the eight FvdW deltahedra actually look like. A reader who hadn't seen the classification before was being asked to take on faith that the polytopes had certain vertex-degree distributions and edge counts. Grok suggested adding an eight-panel figure with one panel per FvdW deltahedron, vertex-degree color-coded, to make the combinatorial structure visible at a glance.

The suggestion was good. The figure was added at v0.8. Each panel showed one FvdW deltahedron projected onto the plane: tetrahedron, triangular bipyramid, octahedron, pentagonal bipyramid, snub disphenoid, triaugmented triangular prism, gyroelongated square bipyramid, icosahedron. Vertices of degree three were drawn green; degree four cyan; degree five red. The reader could see directly, from the color distribution across the eight panels, that degree-five vertices first appear at $N_\alpha = 7$ (the pentagonal bipyramid's two apices) and dominate by $N_\alpha = 12$ (the icosahedron, all twelve vertices red). This is exactly the pattern that explains why strict-C1 fails at $N_\alpha \geq 7$ and refined-C1 is necessary.

The figure shipped at v0.8. It looked correct. ChatGPT's d.7 review caught two errors in it.

The panel showing the octahedron — six vertices, twelve edges, every vertex of degree four — drew only eleven of the twelve edges. The missing edge connected vertex 6 to vertex 4 in the figure's labeling. Two of the vertices were colored cyan (degree four) but actually had degree three in the drawn graph. The mismatch between color and drawn degree was the symptom. The cause was a typo in the TikZ source: a single edge that should have been included in the spokes line had been omitted.

The panel showing the snub disphenoid — eight vertices, eighteen edges, four vertices of degree four and four of degree five — drew only seventeen of the eighteen edges. The missing edge closed a four-vertex cycle on the equator. Same symptom: two vertices colored red (degree five) had degree four in the drawn graph. Same cause: a missing edge in the TikZ source.

I had visually inspected the figure during v0.8 development. The eye did what eyes do: filled in the expected structure even where the page was blank. The bugs were genuinely there in the source, but they were not visible to me on inspection because I knew what the figure was supposed to show. ChatGPT, working from the .tex source rather than from a visual rendering, caught what visual inspection had missed.

The fix was straightforward — two edges added, four lines of TikZ. What followed mattered more than the fix itself. I wrote a programmatic invariant audit: a parser that extracted the figure's combinatorial content from the .tex source (vertex declarations, edge declarations, color codings), computed the drawn degree at each vertex, and compared against both the color-declared degree and the FvdW deltahedron's known invariants ($N_\alpha$, $|E|$, degree distribution). Pre-fix, the audit confirmed exactly the two bugs ChatGPT had flagged and verified that the other six panels were clean. Post-fix, all eight panels passed.

The audit became Lesson 6 of the SS-9 polish track and went into the operating-system documentation as a programme-level discipline: when a paper introduces a non-trivial TikZ figure with combinatorial content, write a parser that verifies the figure against the mathematical invariants it conveys, and run the parser before commit. Visual inspection is insufficient because the eye fills in expected structure. The discipline emerged from a real failure — a figure that shipped at v0.8 with bugs that visual inspection had missed — and now applies to every future CPP figure.

Lesson 6 is what I would not have produced without ChatGPT's d.7 review. The figure would have remained buggy on a release-quality version of the paper. The methodology blind spot — that the eye fills in expected structure — would have remained unidentified. The d.7 review was the seventh AI review pass on the paper, and it caught what the previous six had missed. The cumulative review tally was the test: not any single reviewer's pass, but the aggregate of all of them.

## The Theorem

The five-clause Theorem 6.1 of SS-9 is what the lemmas, the sub-lemmas, the figure, and the cumulative review eventually produced. Stated under the hypothesis stack — refined-C1, C2, C3, C5, C6, C7, C8, rigid packing, and 3D-non-degeneracy — it says:

(i) The alpha-cluster contact graph $G(\mathcal{C})$ is connected.

(ii) The alpha-cluster centroids are non-coplanar in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

(iii) The edge count is $|E(G)| = 3N_\alpha - 6$ exactly.

(iv) For $N_\alpha \in \{4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12\}$ — the FvdW values — $G$ is the 1-skeleton of the unique Freudenthal–van der Waerden convex deltahedron at $N_\alpha$, and the centroid configuration realizes that deltahedron geometrically with edges at uniform contact length $R_{\alpha\alpha}$.

(v) $G$ is planar and triangulated.

The third clause is the simplicial-polytope edge identity that SS-7 had to assume. It is now a *theorem clause*. The first, second, and fifth clauses are the structural conditions (graph connected, three-dimensional, triangulated) that the formula relies on. The fourth clause is the FvdW identification — the alpha cluster, at FvdW values, realizes one of eight specific polyhedra, named.

The theorem does what SS-7's C4 did, and more. SS-7 had a single black-box assumption — that the cluster forms a simplicial 3-polytope — with a specific edge count to use in the formula. SS-9 replaces the black box with a structured derivation that splits C4 into smaller, individually named, individually open conditions: C5, C6, C7, C8. Each is a paper-level structural hypothesis. Each has a corresponding programme-level open problem registered for first-principles closure. OPEN-SS-29 (C5 closure). OPEN-SS-30 (C6 closure). OPEN-SS-33 (C7 closure, advanced via Sub-Lemma 2.1 to "conditionally closed modulo H4 and H5"). OPEN-SS-37 (C8 closure, with four candidate routes registered).

The theorem is conditional. It is honest about being conditional. It is a *conditional theorem closure paper*: a paper whose principal result is a conditional theorem under explicitly registered hypotheses, with the corresponding first-principles-closure questions registered as programme-level open problems. The paper does not claim to derive the FvdW realization unconditionally. It claims to derive it under the registered conditions. A reader who reads the paper as an unconditional derivation is misreading; the paper says, in its title block, in its §9 Roadmap, and in its CHANGELOG, what the conditional structure is.

The simplicial-polytope assumption that SS-7 had to make and SS-8 had to inherit is no longer an assumption SS-9 has to make. It is, conditional on C5 + C6 + C7 + C8, a derivation. The assumption has been split into smaller, more tractable, individually open pieces. Whether those pieces can be each closed from CPP primitives is the programme's next decade of work.

## The Deltahedra Gap

Three values of $N_\alpha$ are missing from the FvdW classification. At $N_\alpha = 11$, $N_\alpha = 13$, and $N_\alpha = 14$, no convex deltahedron exists. The theorem clause (iv), as a result, does not apply at these values. The theorem at $N_\alpha = 11$ guarantees that the cluster is a simplicial 3-polytope (clauses i, ii, iii, v all hold), but does not identify which specific polytope it realizes — because, by FvdW, no convex one exists. Non-convex realizations do extend the list, but the theorem does not speak to non-convex shapes. The empirical agreement at titanium-44 ($N_\alpha = 11$), iron-52 ($N_\alpha = 13$), and nickel-56 ($N_\alpha = 14$) in SS-7 used cluster geometries that are non-convex deltahedra in the broader sense — the deltahedra-gap is the FvdW gap, not the simplicial gap.

The deltahedra-gap registered as OPEN-SS-31. It is a scope-extension target rather than a closure target: the question is whether the SS-9 framework can be extended to cover the non-convex cases at $N_\alpha \in \{11, 13, 14\}$, or whether those three nuclei require a separately argued geometric story. The current paper does not commit to a path. The empirical fits at those three nuclei still hold to within 1.5%, which is the same band as the FvdW values, so the binding-energy formula is empirically silent on the convex-vs-non-convex distinction. The gap is a structural one: the theorem covers eight of the eleven simplicial deltahedra in the FvdW range, not all eleven.

The cluster itself, at $N_\alpha = 11$, is one of several non-convex options: the augmented sphenocorona, the snub square antiprism, or another simplicial 3-polytope without a convex realization. The geometry is not pinned down by the paper; only the edge count is. SS-7's $E = 3N_\alpha - 6$ continues to hold by Euler's formula, and the binding formula continues to give the right answer. Why titanium-44 sits exactly where SS-7's formula predicts, despite the FvdW gap, is part of OPEN-SS-31.

## What v1.0 Means

SS-9 shipped at v1.0 in early May 2026, after seven independent AI review passes — ChatGPT's d.1, Copilot's d.2, ChatGPT's d.3, the cache-resolution d.4, Grok's d.5, Copilot's d.6, ChatGPT's d.7 — all converged on the verdict "v1.0-ready as a conditional theorem closure paper." There was, at the time of ship, no human domain-expert reviewer in Thomas's research network. The paper was advanced to v1.0 on the explicit basis of AI review, not human peer review.

This needed to be said in the paper itself. The v1.0 designation is widely read in academic publishing as carrying particular kinds of validation — peer review, domain-expert engagement, an editorial process. SS-9 had none of those at the time of ship. What it had was seven independent AI review passes, two of which (Copilot's d.6 and ChatGPT's d.7) had explicitly used the words *v1.0-ready as a conditional closure paper* to describe their verdicts. The paper's title block at v1.0 said this directly: *"v1.0 promotion from v0.9 on the explicit basis of seven independent AI review passes; sub-task (e) external/human review rescoped to open invitation post-v1.0 ship via public posting."* The §9 Roadmap subsection added a "Note on the v1.0 designation" that documented the rescope explicitly, named the seven passes, named the venues for the rescoped external review (OSF deposit and arXiv submission as the public-posting channel), and invited domain-expert feedback in named topical areas: nuclear physics, alpha-cluster theory, computational geometry (especially the Euclidean distance matrix, rigidity, and realization-spaces literature relevant to OPEN-SS-37 Route (d)), and discrete mathematics (Steinitz realizations, FvdW classification literature).

The rescope of sub-task (e) — from "blocking gate before v1.0 ship" to "open invitation post-v1.0 ship via public posting" — was a methodology decision that I want to be specific about, because it was the closest I came to a calibration error in the polish track. The original framing had sub-task (e) as a hard gate: no v1.0 ship without external/human review. When it became clear that no human domain-expert reviewer was available in the author's research network, the binary options were (a) hold the paper indefinitely at v0.9, waiting for a human reviewer who might never come, or (b) ship at v1.0 on the AI-review-only basis with the rescope made explicit. The third option — pretending the AI review had been human review — was not actually an option, because the symmetric-honesty discipline that governs the entire programme would have been broken by it. We chose (b). The rescope makes the basis of the v1.0 designation visible to anyone reading the paper. It does not soften it. It says, plainly, *this is a v1.0 conditional theorem closure paper validated by AI review, not by human peer review*, and invites the human review as the next step rather than as a precondition.

I would prefer to have human domain-expert review on a paper like SS-9. The paper is technically substantial and uses mathematical machinery (Steinitz, FvdW, distance-geometry/rigidity-theory connections in Route (d) of OPEN-SS-37) that would benefit from review by people who use that machinery professionally. The public-posting venues are exactly that invitation. The paper sits at github.com/Hyperphysics-Institute/CPP, and at the OSF DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/JXE8D once the deposit lands, and on arXiv once that submission lands. Anyone reading this chapter who has the relevant domain expertise is invited to be the human reviewer. That is what the rescope makes possible.

## What Comes With the Picture

The figure itself, in its corrected post-d.7 form, shows what the eight FvdW polytopes look like and why their vertex-degree distributions matter. A reader holding the figure can see the geometric content of the theorem: that the alpha cluster, at FvdW values, realizes one of eight specific shapes, and that the shapes have specific vertex-degree distributions that determine which alphas in the cluster present which numbers of contact faces.

The conditional-theorem framing has consequences beyond SS-9. It is, I think, the right model for what an unfinished theorem in physics looks like when it ships. The framework of CPP is in active development; its theorems are being produced one at a time, often with hypotheses that are not yet derived from the framework's most basic axioms. A paper that claims unconditional results is not honest about the development state. A paper that registers each conditional dependency, names each open problem, and ships only what it can defend with the current proof — leaving the corresponding closure questions open and visible — is what the methodology actually produces. SS-9 is the first paper in the strong-sector series to ship under this framing explicitly. SS-7 had been conditional in its own way (C4 was registered as a structural hypothesis), but SS-7 did not give the conditional-theorem framing a name. SS-9 named it, and the name now applies retroactively to SS-7 and SS-8 as well as forward to whatever follows.

The four OPEN-SS-* registries that the proof opens — OPEN-SS-29 (C5), OPEN-SS-30 (C6), OPEN-SS-33 (C7, partially closed via Sub-Lemma 2.1), OPEN-SS-37 (C8, with four candidate closure routes including the distance-geometry / Euclidean distance matrix / rigidity theory / realization-spaces approach that ChatGPT's d.4 review forwarded as Route (d)) — are the programme's standing problem set in the strong sector going forward. Each of them is a closure question with a name and a status field. OPEN-SS-37's Route (d), in particular, connects the C8 first-principles question to recognized mathematical programmes. The Cayley–Menger determinants of Schoenberg's distance-matrix theory; the Maxwell–Cremona tensegrity correspondence and the Asimow–Roth and Laman conditions in rigidity theory; the alpha complexes of Edelsbrunner; the realization spaces of Mnëv and Richter-Gebert. These are not CPP-internal constructions. They are existing mathematical machinery that the C8 question turns out to be exactly the right kind of question for. Whether one of those programmes will yield C8 from CPP's primitives is what the next round of work is for.

## Closing

The geometers found the right shapes a long time before the physicists found the right place to put them — that was where SS-8 ended. SS-9 makes the bridge across that gap explicit, and shows what its conditions are.

Steinitz's theorem is from 1922. It says, with a precision that has not been improved in a century, exactly which graphs are 1-skeletons of convex 3-polytopes. The Freudenthal–van der Waerden classification is from 1947. It says, with similar precision, exactly which 3-polytopes have all-triangular faces. CPP, in 2026, is not adding to either. It is finding that its primitives — the conscious points, the dipole pair sea, the 600-cell substrate, the K₃ collective-mode quantum, the alpha-cage geometry — converge with what the geometers had already established. The convergence is not coincidental; it is what *should* happen if both pictures are right, because both pictures are about the same physical reality.

But the convergence is conditional. SS-9 does not derive the FvdW realization unconditionally. It derives it under nine specific conditions, four of which — C5, C6, C7, C8 — are not yet themselves derivable from the framework's primitives. The paper is honest about this, in five separate places. The chapter is honest about it here. The conditions are named, registered, and visible. They are not hidden under fluent prose or buried in footnotes. They are exactly what is being asked of the reader to assume, and exactly what the programme's next decade of work is for.

That, finally, is what the v1.0 designation in this case has to mean. Not that the paper has been ratified by the standard structures of academic peer review — it has not, because the structures were not available at the time of ship. Not that the result is unconditional — it is not, because the conditions are visible. The v1.0 designation means that the paper is ready for external scrutiny under its registered conditions. It means that what the paper claims to deliver, it can defend; and what the paper does not claim, it does not assert. Anyone reading the chapter who has the mathematics or the physics to engage with the proof is invited to be the human reviewer. The paper is on GitHub. The OSF deposit and the arXiv submission are the next step.

Steinitz waited a hundred years for someone to need his theorem in nuclear physics. Freudenthal and van der Waerden waited eighty. The theorem and the classification have been there all along, unchanged, waiting. The framework's job is to produce the conditions under which they apply. C5 and C6 and C7 and C8 are what is left. The geometers were not asking the physicists to do anything except meet them where they had been waiting.
