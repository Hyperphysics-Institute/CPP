# The Charm That Was Already There

There are about twenty numbers in the Standard Model of particle physics that nobody can explain. They are not wrong, and they are not approximate. They are measured to exquisite precision and they work. But they are *put in by hand* — the masses of the quarks and leptons, the strengths of the forces, the angles that govern how one kind of particle turns into another. A physicist who wants to know the mass of the charm quark does not derive it. She looks it up. Somebody measured it, and the number — about 1.27 billion electron-volts, a little more than a proton and a third — went into the table next to all the others.

This is not a scandal. A theory is allowed to have inputs. Newton did not derive the mass of the Earth; he measured it and got everything else right. But the quark masses have always had a teasing quality, because they are not random-looking. They climb in a pattern — up, charm, top, each generation heavier than the last by a large and somewhat regular factor — and patterns invite the question of whether the numbers are really independent or only look that way. For fifty years the honest answer has been: we don't know. We measure them one at a time and write them down.

This chapter is about a paper that did not derive those numbers either — and that is the first thing to be clear about. The quark masses in Conscious Point Physics were derived earlier, in other papers, by other arguments. What this paper, SF-3, did was assemble those scattered results into a single account of the quark sector, and in doing so it ran into a small, sharp question about bookkeeping. The question was whether the theory needed to measure the charm mass at all, or whether it had been quietly predicting it the whole time while pretending to measure it. The answer turned out to matter more than a bookkeeping question usually does.

## Why the quarks resist explanation

It is not as though physics has ignored these numbers for fifty years. The attempts to explain them are part of what makes the column interesting, and part of why a new claim about it has to be made carefully.

The most famous hint came from Yoshio Koide, who noticed in 1983 that the three charged-lepton masses — electron, muon, tau — obey a relationship of almost suspicious precision: a particular combination of their square roots comes out to exactly two-thirds, to within the experimental error, and stays there as the measurements improve. Nobody knows why. It has the flavour of a clue left at a crime scene with no detective able to read it. Over the decades physicists have built machinery to coax the mass pattern out of deeper structure — symmetries that forbid certain entries in the mass tables and force relationships among the rest, cascades in which heavy particles lend mass to lighter ones through a hierarchy of couplings, and, at the pessimistic end, the suggestion that the masses are simply random draws and we should stop looking for a pattern. Each of these captures something. None of them predicts the numbers from nothing.

And there is a second puzzle, quieter than the masses but in some ways more pointed. The strong force and the electroweak force are described by separate theories with separate strengths, and the Standard Model offers no reason for those strengths to be related. The number that sets the strong force and the angle that governs electroweak mixing are measured in different experiments, decades apart, by different communities. That they should have anything to do with each other is not a question most physicists would think to ask, because the framework gives no reason to ask it. Hold onto that; it is where this sector has its quietest surprise.

CPP's contribution to all of this is not that it dethrones the earlier work. The symmetry models are real; the cascade pictures produce real results; Koide's relation is a genuine and still-unexplained regularity. What the quark-sector paper claims is narrower and more specific, and the chapter's honesty depends on keeping the two straight.

## What was already on the table

By the time the quark-sector paper was written, the pieces existed. They had simply never been put in one place.

A few years of work in the CPP programme had produced a picture in which a quark is not a point but a *cage* — a small, colour-charged configuration of the theory's underlying entities, sitting on one of the discrete distance-shells of a four-dimensional crystal called the 600-cell. The 600-cell is a real object, a regular polytope in four dimensions, the four-dimensional cousin of the icosahedron, and it has been known to geometers since the nineteenth century. In CPP it is the scaffolding of space itself. The quarks live on its shells the way notes live on the frets of a string: only certain positions are allowed, and each allowed position has a definite size.

Four of those shells are bonded — stable enough to hold a quark cage — and they have vertex counts of 4, 12, 20, and 30. These four numbers are the down-type and up-type heavy quarks: strange, charm, bottom, top. The mass of a cage was shown, in an earlier paper, to scale with its vertex count $V$ in a specific way:

$$ M_q = m_e \left(\frac{z}{\varphi}\right) V^{7/3}. $$

There is a lot packed into that line, and almost none of it is adjustable. $m_e$ is the electron mass — the one and only dimensionful number the whole construction is calibrated against. $z = 12$ is how many nearest neighbours a point has in the 600-cell. $\varphi$ is the golden ratio, $1.618\ldots$, which appears throughout the geometry of the icosahedron and its four-dimensional relatives the way $\pi$ appears throughout circles. The exponent $7/3$ was itself derived, in yet another paper, by counting how the cage's stored energy grows as it gets bigger. The top quark carries one extra factor — a relay of its energy across all twelve neighbours, weighted by a colour factor — that comes out to exactly sixteen.

So the masses were there. So was the strength of the strong force, $\alpha_s$, which an earlier paper had identified with a particular fraction of the crystal's vibrational spectrum, $5/(8\varphi)$, about $0.386$. So was a striking relationship between the strong force and the weak force that I will come back to, because it is one of the small miracles of the sector. And so was the colour symmetry $SU(3)$ — the algebra Gell-Mann and Ne'eman fit to the particle data in 1961 without knowing where it came from — derived here from the permutation symmetry of a cage's faces.

Six papers. The quark sector was, in a real sense, finished. It had just never been written down as a quark sector. That was the job: assembly. And assembly, done honestly, is mostly a matter of deciding what to keep when two earlier papers quietly disagree.

## Two routes to the same masses

They disagreed about the charm mass. Not about its value — both got it right — but about its *status*.

One route, from the cage-formula paper, predicted all four heavy-quark masses from the formula above. Strange, charm, bottom, top, all four, from $m_e$ and the geometry, with no further inputs. The root-mean-square error across the four masses, spanning four orders of magnitude from the strange quark to the top, was 2.1 percent. In this route the charm mass is an *output*: the formula says 1249 MeV, and the measured value is about 1270, so the prediction is low by 1.6 percent. Charm is predicted, not assumed.

The other route, from the strong-coupling paper, did something subtler and, on its own terms, slightly more accurate. It used the charm mass as a *calibration* — a fixed reference point — to anchor a quantity called the quark Koide phase, and in exchange it got marginally better agreement on the bottom and top masses, errors around 1.4 to 1.7 percent instead of the cage formula's larger residuals. The cost was that it now had two calibrations instead of one: the electron mass, and the charm mass.

This is the kind of fork that decides what a paper is. One route is cleaner; the other is more accurate. A careless synthesis takes the more accurate numbers and quietly accepts the second calibration, because who would turn down better agreement with experiment? The reason not to is that the second calibration is *redundant*. The cage formula already derives the charm mass from the electron mass. If you have a prediction for charm, you do not also need to measure charm and feed it back in as an input. You can let the first route supply the number the second route was treating as given.

So the decision was to demote the charm mass — to move it from the column labelled "measured inputs" to the column labelled "predictions," and to keep a single calibration, $m_e$, for the entire quark sector. The price was real: the masses are now known to 2.1 percent rather than the slightly-better figure the two-calibration route achieved. About a third of a percent was given back on the bottom and top.

It was worth it, and not for a reason internal to the quark sector. The charged leptons (electron, muon, tau) had been derived in their own paper from the same single calibration. The neutrinos had too. If the quarks also rest on $m_e$ alone, then a later paper — the one that ties all of these together into an account of why the particle masses span the range they do — can put a single line at the top of its master table: *one calibration, for every fermion mass in the Standard Model.* That line is the spine of the whole argument. A third of a percent of accuracy is not worth bending it.

This is the moment I would most have liked to oversell. It is tempting to write "CPP predicts the charm quark mass" in bold and move on. But the honest statement is quieter and stranger: the charm mass had been a prediction all along, and the earlier paper had been using it as a calibration anyway, out of an abundance of caution, paying for a precision it did not need with a parameter it could not afford. Recognizing that the knob was redundant was the work. The number did not change. What changed was our understanding of whether we were allowed to claim it.

## The phase that did not depend on charm

There was an objection waiting, and it is worth following because it is where the paper's reasoning is sharpest.

The quark Koide phase is a number — about 124 degrees — that describes a hidden regularity in how the three generations of quark masses are spaced. It is the same Koide regularity that organizes the charged leptons, the one from 1983, expressed now through a phase angle and applied to the quarks. In the second route, this phase was where the charm calibration did its work. So when we demoted charm, the natural worry was: doesn't the phase now have to be recomputed against the *derived* charm mass? Hasn't pulling charm out of the inputs knocked over the thing the inputs were holding up?

The outline of the paper, written early, assumed exactly this — that the phase machinery would need to be "re-grounded" on the new, derived charm mass. Working through it carefully showed that the re-grounding step is not needed, and the reason is a small piece of algebra that I want to state plainly because it is the load-bearing observation of the synthesis.

The phase is set by a formula that involves the strong coupling, the weak mixing angle, and the coordination number $z$ — and nothing else. In particular it does not involve the overall mass scale. In the Koide description, each mass is written as an overall amplitude $A_q$ times a factor that depends on the phase. The charm mass entered the earlier construction *only* through that amplitude $A_q$ — and the amplitude is an overall multiplier that cancels out of the ratio that fixes the phase. It divides out. The phase never saw the charm mass at all; it saw only the geometry.

I registered this as Proposition 5.1 of the paper, and I was careful — for reasons that became important — to label it as what it is: a *bookkeeping separation*, not a new theorem. It does not derive anything. It observes that two quantities the earlier work had entangled were never actually entangled, so that demoting charm leaves the phase untouched. The synthesis is simpler than a patchwork: the masses come entirely from the cage formula, and the phase comes entirely from the geometry, and the two do not lean on each other.

That would have been the end of it, except that this is the part of the work where the AI review made the difference between an argument that was true and an argument that was *defensibly* true.

The CPP papers are reviewed, before they ship, by a panel of AI systems — at present ChatGPT, xAI's Grok, and Microsoft's Copilot — each reading the full draft and pushing on it independently, across several rounds. Through four rounds of this, Grok returned a clean verdict each time and the others converged from "revise" to "ship." But in the final round one of the adversarial passes caught something I had stated too loosely. My proposition said the phase did not depend on the charm mass. The reviewer pointed out that this is true *only because* the strong coupling is being taken as its structural value, $5/(8\varphi)$ — a pure number from the geometry. If instead you took the strong coupling from the usual experimental procedure, where it is extracted from measurements made at an energy scale set by the charm mass itself, then the charm mass would creep back in through the side door, through the coupling, and the independence would quietly fail.

The paper uses the structural value, so the proposition holds. But the unstated version invited exactly the objection a hostile referee lives for. Adding one sentence — *the independence holds because $\alpha_s$ is the structural value, not a charm-scale fit* — closed the door. It is a small edit. It is also the difference between a claim that survives scrutiny and one that looks airtight until someone who knows where renormalization hides leans on it. I would not have found it on my own in that round; the panel did, and the paper is more honest for it.

That catch was the sharpest of several. Across the four rounds the panel walked the paper's language back, one notch at a time, away from claims the work could not quite support. An early draft said the geometry *forced* exactly three generations; the reviewers pointed out that the underlying argument selects three generations within a particular model of how the crystal folds onto itself, which is a weaker and more honest statement, and the verb went from "forced" to "selected by the mechanism" to, finally, "selected within the model." The word "unification," used loosely for the strong–weak relationship, was softened to "correspondence." A sentence claiming the paper handled the *complete* quark sector was narrowed to the *heavy* quarks, since the lightest two were not its subject. None of these were errors of physics; they were errors of register, the kind of small overconfidence that creeps into a synthesis when the author can see how well the pieces fit and wants the reader to see it too. Stripping them out is most of what the review process did, and the paper that shipped says less, and means it more, than the paper that was first drafted.

## One spectrum, two forces

The single most beautiful thing in the sector is not the masses. It is a relationship between two numbers that physics has always treated as having nothing to do with each other.

The strong force has a strength, $\alpha_s$. The electroweak theory has an angle, the Weinberg angle, whose sine-squared is about $0.23$ — it sets how the electromagnetic and weak forces mix. These are measured in completely different kinds of experiments and there is no reason, in the Standard Model, for them to be related. They are two independent inputs from the table of twenty.

In CPP they are two fractions of one thing. The vibrational spectrum of the 600-cell — the catalogue of ways the crystal can ring — divides into modes that live on the *edges* of the structure and modes that live on the *faces*. The weak mixing angle is the share carried by the edge modes; the strong coupling is the share carried by the face modes. And because they are two slices of one pie, they add to the whole:

$$ \sin^2\theta_W + \alpha_s = \frac{1}{\varphi}. $$

The two numbers physicists measured separately — $0.232$ and $0.386$ — sum to $0.618$, which is one over the golden ratio, to the precision the inputs allow. Their ratio is $5/3$, which is the ratio of the number of faces to the number of edges in the relevant count. Two forces, one spectrum.

I want to be careful here, because this is the other place the chapter could overreach. These are *structural correspondences*, not derivations of the couplings from the dynamics of the forces. The geometry produces two pure numbers; those numbers match the measured strong coupling and weak angle; the match is striking and it is zero-parameter. But it is not the same thing as deriving the running of the strong coupling from a quantum field theory. The honest word is *correspondence*. The reviewers pressed on this too, and the paper says so in plain language. The miracle, if it is one, is that two quantities with no Standard-Model relationship turn out to be complementary slices of a single object. Whether that is a deep fact about nature or a structural coincidence the reader is invited to weigh, the chapter's job is only to state it precisely enough that the weighing is possible.

The same machinery that produces these two fractions produces the quark Koide phase. The phase is the angle by which the colour and electroweak self-energy of a quark cage rotates a particular eigenvalue away from its bare geometric value of $-2/3$. Run the arithmetic and it lands at 124.04 degrees. The value extracted from the measured quark masses is 124.09 degrees. They agree to five hundredths of a percent. The number was not fit to anything; it fell out of the same spectrum that gave the two couplings.

## What the synthesis bought, and what it left open

Put the pieces in one place and the quark sector reads like this. One calibration, the electron mass. Zero adjustable shape parameters. From those, the four heavy-quark masses to about two percent; the strong coupling as a pure number that matches experiment; its exact complementarity with the weak angle; the Koide phase to a twentieth of a percent; and the count of exactly three generations, with no fourth quark — selected, within the crystal's antipodal-identification model, by the same geometry that fixes everything else. A fourth-generation quark would falsify it cleanly; none has been found, and the bounds are already tight.

There is one large thing the sector does not do, and the paper says so on its own terms rather than burying it. It does not derive the CKM matrix — the set of angles that govern how quarks of one generation transform into quarks of another, and the single phase inside that matrix that is responsible for the difference between matter and antimatter in the quark sector. There is no derivation of those numbers anywhere in the CPP quark corpus. I registered the gap formally, as an open problem, and framed it honestly: it is the structural twin of a gap already standing open in the neutrino sector, where the masses are derived but the corresponding mixing phase is not. The neutrino paper shipped seven of its eight parameters and left the eighth open. The quark paper ships the masses and the generation count and leaves the mixing open. The parallel is one of *posture* — masses derived, mixing deferred — not a claim that the two gaps are equally hard. There is a candidate route, an analogy to how the neutrino mixing was eventually pinned down by a spectral-alignment argument, but it is unscoped, and an honest paper says "unscoped" rather than gesturing at a solution it does not have.

This is, in the end, what a synthesis paper is for. It did not add a number to the world. It took six papers that each knew part of the quark sector and showed that they fit together on a single calibration — and in the fitting, it found that one of the calibrations the earlier work had been carrying was a prediction in disguise. The charm mass was already there, in the geometry, the whole time. The earlier paper had been measuring something the theory already knew.

There is a temptation, looking at a result like this, to feel that the bookkeeping is too convenient — that a theory which can demote its own inputs whenever they become inconvenient is a theory that can never be wrong. The discipline that answers the temptation is the one the paper tried to hold to throughout: every demotion has to be paid for in the open. Demoting charm cost a third of a percent of accuracy, and the paper reports the worse number rather than the better one. The phase-independence claim cost a sentence of conditional honesty, supplied by a reviewer who would not let it pass otherwise. The mixing matrix is simply marked unsolved. A theory earns the right to consolidate its inputs only by being this explicit about what each consolidation costs.

The quarks have been, for fifty years, a column of numbers measured one at a time. What this paper claims is narrower and stranger than a derivation of that column. It claims that the column was never as wide as it looked — that most of its entries were shadows of one number and one shape, and that the charm mass, which everyone had been measuring, was a thing the geometry had been quietly telling us all along, if anyone had thought to stop writing it down and ask whether it was already known.
