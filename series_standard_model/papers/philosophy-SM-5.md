## I. The Question Behind SM-5

### Why do neutrinos mix so much?

The most puzzling feature of the PMNS matrix, from the Standard Model's perspective, is the large mixing angles. In the quark sector, the CKM matrix is close to the identity — quarks of different generations mix only a little. In the lepton sector, the PMNS matrix is far from the identity — almost "maximally mixed" in the θ₂₃ direction. This difference is unexplained in the Standard Model; it is simply observed.

Several theoretical frameworks have proposed geometric origins for the large lepton mixing angles. The A₄ discrete symmetry approach identifies a tetrahedral symmetry group and shows that it predicts TBM mixing. But A₄ is postulated ad hoc for this purpose — it has no explanation within the Standard Model or any known physics.

CPP's question is different: does the geometric structure we already have — the K3 cage base derived from the 600-cell — naturally produce large neutrino mixing, and if so, why?

### The answer: it is the mismatch between two natural bases

The TBM mixing matrix is, in CPP, not a separate postulate or a separate symmetry. It is the matrix that converts between two bases that are both naturally defined by the K3 structure:

- The **vertex basis** is the basis in which charged leptons are diagonal. Each generation is a vertex: V₁ (electron), V₂ (muon), V₃ (tau).
- The **eigenmode basis** is the basis in which the K3 Hamiltonian is diagonal. Each neutrino mass eigenstate is an eigenmode: φ₋^(1) (ν₁), φ₊ (ν₂), φ₋^(2) (ν₃).

These two bases are not aligned. They cannot be — the vertex basis is maximally localised (each state at one vertex), while the eigenmode basis is maximally delocalised (each state over all three vertices). The misalignment between "maximally localised" and "maximally delocalised" is what produces the large mixing angles.

This is the geometric intuition behind TBM in CPP: large neutrino mixing is a consequence of the fact that neutrinos and charged leptons use complementary bases of the same geometric object.

## II. The Physical Significance of the Vertex/Eigenmode Distinction

### Why do charged leptons use the vertex basis?

Charged leptons are vertex excitations because they carry electric charge. In CPP, electric charge is sourced by an unpaired CP at a specific cage vertex — the central eCP of the electron sits at a specific point in the cage structure. Its generation identity (electron vs. muon vs. tau) is determined by which vertex of K3 it predominantly occupies in its ZBW trajectory, which in turn determines the thermal occupation distribution (SM-3, SM-4). The charge-sourcing CP localises the lepton to a definite vertex.

### Why do neutrinos use the eigenmode basis?

Neutrinos carry no electric charge and no colour charge. They do not source SSV gradients at specific vertices. In CPP, a particle that sources no localised SSV is not pinned to any vertex — it propagates as a global mode of the cage structure, coupling to all three vertices simultaneously. The natural basis for such a particle is the eigenmode basis: the modes that are eigenstates of the cage Hamiltonian and therefore propagate with a definite frequency.

This is the physical motivation for the ansatz. It is not a postulate without motivation — it follows from the CPP rules for how different types of CPs couple to cage structures. The full derivation (registering as OP-SM-nu-id) requires formalising these rules for neutral, colourless particles, which requires the electroweak sector of CPP.

### The deeper principle: locality determines basis

The vertex/eigenmode distinction in SM-5 is an instance of a general principle in CPP: the basis in which a particle's mass eigenstates are defined is determined by the degree of locality of the particle's coupling to the cage structure. Highly localised coupling → vertex basis. Delocalised coupling → eigenmode basis.

This principle has direct consequences for the mixing structure. A particle that couples locally is in a definite vertex state: it has a definite "address" in the cage. Its mass eigenstates are the same as its address eigenstates. A particle that couples globally is in a superposition of vertex states: it has no definite address, only a definite oscillation mode. Its mass eigenstates are the eigenmodes, which are orthogonal to the vertex states in a specific way — and that specific way is the TBM matrix.

The large neutrino mixing angles, on this picture, are not a mystery requiring a new symmetry. They are the natural consequence of the fact that neutrinos are globally coupled while charged leptons are locally coupled, and the mismatch between these two coupling styles is measured by the TBM matrix.

## IV. The Ansatz as a Philosophical Statement

### Honesty as a theoretical virtue

The explicit labelling of the neutrino identification as an ansatz is more than scientific caution. It is a philosophical statement about the relationship between mathematical results and physical derivations.

In many theoretical papers, assumptions are hidden in the formalism. A symmetry is "imposed" without explanation. A coupling is "chosen" to reproduce known results. The assumption is there, but it is not named as an assumption. The reader must detect it.

SM-5 does the opposite. The central assumption is named, labelled, and registered as an open problem in the first line of Proposition 2.1. The reader knows exactly where the paper's foundational claim is and exactly what would need to be done to convert it from an ansatz to a theorem.

This transparency is a theoretical virtue for two reasons. First, it is honest: the paper claims to prove exactly what it proves, not more. Second, it is constructive: by identifying the open problem precisely (derive why neutrinos use eigenmodes rather than vertex states), it sets a clear agenda for the next phase of the CPP neutrino programme.

### The value of conditional theorems

A conditional theorem — "if X, then Y" — is a genuine theorem even when X is not yet established. The history of mathematics and physics is full of conditional theorems that were proved before their conditions were established, and whose proof motivated the subsequent derivation of the conditions.

The Riemann hypothesis is the most famous example: an enormous amount of mathematics has been developed "assuming the Riemann hypothesis," and this conditional development has been productive even though the hypothesis is unproved. In physics, the development of string theory in 10 dimensions was partially motivated by the conditional theorem that anomaly cancellation requires exactly 10 dimensions.

SM-5's conditional theorem — "if neutrinos are eigenmode excitations, then U_PMNS^(0) = U_TBM" — is in this tradition. It is a genuine theorem with a clear condition. The condition is the next research problem. The theorem's validity does not depend on the condition being established; it depends only on the condition being clearly stated, which it is.

### 3. What does the vertex/eigenmode distinction tell us about the nature of mass?

In SM-5, mass plays two different roles for charged leptons and neutrinos. For charged leptons, mass is determined by vertex occupation statistics (SM-3, SM-4): the generation identity determines which vertex is predominantly occupied, and the Koide relation governs the mass ratios. The mass of a particle, in CPP, is not a fixed intrinsic property but is determined by how the particle couples to the cage structure — and that coupling depends on the particle's charge and colour structure.

This is a more dynamic view of mass than the Standard Model's Higgs mechanism provides. In the Standard Model, mass is a coupling constant between a field and the Higgs field — fundamentally static. In CPP, mass emerges from the dynamics of ZBW occupation, which depends on the particle's relationship to the cage geometry. The same cage produces different mass structures for particles with different coupling types.
