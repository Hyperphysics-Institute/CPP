## 3. The one load-bearing claim: "the same duration" (script)

Everything turns on his "repeated increments of the same … duration". For a sharp line, every realignment must take the same time, so that the waves come at
one steady rate and in step, with no gaps (4471 §3). But the realignment is driven by "the forces acting between DPs", which "change after each discharge". As the
cloud empties, those forces change (Claude reads them as weakening; his text says only "change"), and a realignment driven by changing forces would generally
change its time, longer or shorter: the Coulomb-timed swing of 4460 §5 speeds up as it drains. If it changes, the rate drifts through the emission and the line
smears (a chirp).

A toy model (not CPP dynamics) puts a number on it. Let the realignment time depend on the energy left, as (energy left)^(−p); p = 0 is a fixed clock:

| How the realignment time depends on the energy left | Line width (2,000-cycle toy photon) |
|---|---|
| fixed (p = 0) | 8 × 10⁻⁵, the width from the fading alone |
| p = 0.0001 | 2 × 10⁻⁴ |
| p = 0.001 | 1 × 10⁻³ |
| p = 0.01 | 6 × 10⁻³ |
| p = 0.5 | 0.3 |
| p = −0.001 (speeds up) | 1 × 10⁻³ |

For a real Lyman-α photon, about 3.9 million cycles, the drift must stay within the natural width: |p| of order 4 × 10⁻⁸ or less (at that value the line is already roughly doubled; the toy runs p > 0
mostly; p < 0 smears alike). So **the realignment time must stay fixed
to about four parts in a hundred million for each e-fold the cloud's energy falls**, however much the cloud has emptied. In standard physics the requirement
is met because the atom's charge cloud, caught between two states, oscillates at exactly ΔE/h however much of each state it holds; the mixture sets the
strength, not the rate (4460 §5; a fact for CPP to match, not evidence for CPP). The mechanical analogue is a mass on a spring, whose period does not depend on
how far it swings (4451 §3; Claude's harmonic candidate, 4461 §4). In his picture, what makes
the realignment time independent of the remaining forces is the question (§6). Asserting "the same duration" is not yet deriving it. And since each increment is one wave, the realignment time is the period itself: it must also equal
h ÷ ΔE, the same in every atom, so the cloud's own dynamics must set it from the energy gap (= 4472 a, 4470 b).

## 4. "The same energy" is not needed, and is not what is measured

His increments are "of the same energy and duration". The duration must be the same (§3); the energy need not be. A sharp line needs only a steady rate. And the
measured fading, inferred to be each photon's own (4462 §2, 4463 §3, with the caveats there), is exponential: each increment a fixed share of what is left, so
the increments shrink as the cloud empties. Equal increments until the cloud is spent would give a flat envelope that stops abruptly, and a line with side lobes
(4451 §3, 4461 §3.2). His own picture fits the shrinking version naturally: as the cloud empties, fewer arcs are left to come into line, so each portion is
smaller. (Claude's reading.) This refines 4472 §5: hf ÷ N is the *average* share; under exponential fading the early shares are larger and the late ones
smaller. "The same energy" may also be his way of saying the same colour (E = hf per increment), which is 4472(h)'s open question, not a claim about size; on
that reading nothing here contradicts him.

## 5. Can it be simulated? In principle, yes; it needs CPP's rules first

An FEM-type or many-body simulation of the cloud is possible in principle, but it can only follow rules that are stated. To simulate his sequence one would need
at least:
- **the force between two DP arcs** as a function of their separation and orientation;
- **how an arc turns and moves** under those forces (its inertia, which in CPP is the reaction of the DP-arcs a CP's motion organises, each CP advancing by its SSV_net (V_i) each Moment:
  R-CP-NO-REST-MASS; with the 4404 tension noted there);
- **the discharge rule:** when an arc is "in the orientation to allow" discharge, how much it gives up, and in what form;
- **how a discharge radiates** into the photon (the CPP radiation rule, 4465 a);
- **the cloud's starting state:** how many arcs, their positions and orientations, as formed around the orbiting CP.

The registry has the CP-level rules: the relay (A3′/AP-4, 4355), the CP's advance by its SSV_net each Moment with inertia from its DP-arcs
(R-CP-NO-REST-MASS), and DP orientation toward an unpaired CP (R-DPSEA-UNIFORM). It has no arc-level rules (the force between arcs, their turning, discharge,
radiation); these would have to be derived from the CP-level rules, since a simulation at the Planck scale of a whole atom is out of reach. A toy simulation
with assumed rules would test the assumptions, not CPP. So the simulation is owed (Claude's decision under PD-006: the first deliverable is the arc-level rule
set, derived by Claude from the registered rules; the founder's physical picture of how one arc pushes another is asked as a check on that derivation, §6). For
the arcs' contributions to add as one wave they must also be in phase across the cloud: in step, without gaps (4471 g, 4472 g).

## 8. Answer to his "What do you think?"

It's a coherent picture, and it answers the question I asked: once the central CP is knocked out, the cloud loses its organizer, a portion of the arcs that
happens to be lined up with the photon's direction lets go, the changed forces turn the rest, and the next portion comes into line. Each discharge sets up the
next, so the cloud times itself. That's the kind of clock the sharp line needs.

Everything hangs on one phrase: "the same duration." For a sharp line, every realignment has to take the same time. But the forces doing the turning come from
the cloud itself, and they change as it empties, so you'd generally expect each realignment to take a slightly different time from the last, longer or shorter. If it does, the colour
drifts and the line smears. In a simple model, for hydrogen's ultraviolet line, the realignment time can't change by more than about four parts in a hundred
million for every time the cloud's energy falls by about two-thirds. So something has to make the turning time independent of how much is left, the way a
mass on a spring keeps the same period as its swing dies away. And since each piece is one wave, that turning time has to be exactly one period of the colour,
the same in every atom.

"The same energy" isn't needed, and the measured fading says the pieces get smaller, a share of what's left each time, which fits his picture naturally: fewer
arcs are left to come into line. On a simulation: yes, in principle, but it needs CPP's rules first, above all how one DP arc pushes and turns another. The record has
the rules for single points but not for whole arcs; I'll work those out from the point rules first, and I've asked him two things: why the arcs would take the same time to realign when the forces have
changed, and how one arc's push depends on how close and how lined up the two are.
