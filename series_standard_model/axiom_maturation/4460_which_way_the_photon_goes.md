## 1. His answer

- **The pattern and the direction:** like a radio antenna's, the radiation is cylindrical around the axis. But because the discharge was initiated by a

- **The sideways reach:** the cylinder extends as far as the CP's light-speed sideways influence reached during the driven oscillation; the photon has a
  cylindrical radius of effect around its axis. He gives it as "a 1/2 meter radius", and the photon "passes through the 1/2 meter-wide space between slits"
  and interferes with itself.
- **The number of cycles:** the duration depends on how many cycles it completes; "it is possible that there is only one oscillation", its amplitude
  corresponding to the energy difference, "each color" a different amplitude.
- **Why it radiates:** the CP is driven by the KE (DP arc-separation), no longer associated with the orbital energy difference and out of resonance with the

## 3. Which way the photon goes (script part 1)

**A dipole sends nothing along its own axis.** Its radiated strength goes as sin²θ from the swing axis: none along the axis, a quarter at 30°, three quarters
at 60°, most at right angles. That is the antenna's pattern: a doughnut around the wire, spreading outward as a sphere. It is "cylindrical" in the sense of
all round the axis, not a cylinder of fixed radius.

So for the CP's swing through the nucleus:
- **If the photon goes along the swing axis**, it goes the one way a dipole does not radiate. The displacement would then be along the travel, longitudinal,
  as the 2026-07-28 ruling has it, and would carry no polarization.
- **If the photon goes across the swing**, the dipole radiates there most strongly, and the displacement, along the swing, lies across the photon's path.
  That gives polarization along the swing axis, as measured for light from a dipole. But it makes the DP displacement **transverse** to the travel, which
  would revise the reading of the 2026-07-28 ruling (DP arc displacement longitudinal; 4444, 4455). In 4455 he withdrew "transverse CP displacement" as
  meaning the fields, and described the DP separation as "front, back and side". The across branch would put the CP's own swing, and the displacement along
  it, across the travel. That reopens what 4455 closed, unless "side" in 4455 already allows it. Which it is, is his call; it is an axiom-level reading.

**How the collision fits.** In 4446 he put the photon at right angles to the swing and "collinear with the collision vector". That needs a collision that
sets the CP swinging across its own line (4446 d, still open: a kick normally drives motion along the kick). In 4460 the collision's forward impulse drives
the CP. If that drives the swing along the collision, the photon follows the swing axis, where a dipole does not radiate. So the deciding question is which
way the kick sets the swing (§6).

Claude's reading, marked: the across branch is the one that fits the dipole picture he chose; it is not the convenient one for the registry, since it
reopens the ruling.

**Is the direction the collision's? For single atoms and dilute gases, the measurements say not.** This revises Claude's 4446 agreement in outline that
"the collision picks which way".
- **Resonance fluorescence:** atoms excited by a laser from one side re-emit in all directions, not mainly forward along the laser. A single trapped atom
  (Darquié 2005, Beugnon 2006; 4452, 4453) sends its photons into all directions, and a lens catches only the share of directions it covers.
- **Laser cooling and radiation pressure** work only because the recoil of the re-emitted photons averages to zero: the atom is pushed along the laser by
  what it absorbs, and the emission, spread all round, does not undo it. If emission went along the absorbed photon, there would be no net push.
- **Electron-impact excitation:** a gas excited by an electron beam glows all round the beam. The beam's direction shows up in the light's polarization
  and in a gentle variation of brightness with angle (impact polarization), not in a forward beam.
- **The exception:** in optically dense, extended samples there is a coherent forward-directed emission (forward scattering, timed-Dicke superradiance).
  That is a phase effect over many atoms, not one atom following its excitation.

So an impulse can set the swing's axis, and with it the polarization and the dipole pattern around it. It does not send a single atom's photon along the
impulse. Each photon's direction is, as far as experiment shows, chance within the dipole pattern (4446 e). §6 asks what sets it in his picture.

## 4. Half a metre, and one oscillation (script parts 2–3)

**His half metre matches light speed × the emission time.** Hydrogen's 2p state (the Lyman-α line) lives 1.6 ns; light goes 0.48 m in that time. So a
sideways reach set by the CP's light-speed influence during the swing comes out near his half metre **if the swing lasts the lifetime**, about 4 million
cycles. That is also the photon's length along its travel (4448). His figure may echo the 54 cm of 4444 (an atom interferometer's path separation); the
match to c × τ holds for Lyman-α only (the sodium D line, 16 ns, gives about 5 m) and is Claude's reading. It is convenient, since it makes his number come
out, and is marked so (§9).

**One oscillation would give 0.12 μm**, not half a metre: a single Lyman-α cycle lasts 0.41 fs. And one cycle cannot be one colour (4450): a one-cycle pulse
spreads over a band 0.8 times its own frequency (script), while Lyman-α's natural width (from its lifetime) is 1.0 × 10⁸ Hz, 4 parts in 10⁸ of its
frequency. A grating sorts light by the spacing of its crests; one cycle has no spacing to sort by. And photons of one colour all carry the same energy,
h × frequency (4456), so the colour cannot be an amplitude alone. **So, on this reading, his half metre argues for the many-cycle swing, not the single
oscillation.**

Lab double slits are fractions of a millimetre apart, well inside either reach.

## 5. What keeps the rate steady (script part 4)

A swing through the nucleus under the Coulomb pull keeps time by its energy (Kepler's third law: the rate goes as the energy to the 3/2 power, the same as
a circular orbit of that energy, whether the swing bounces or passes through, which only halves all rates). As the swing drains from the n = 2 energy to
the n = 1 energy, its rate would sweep from 8.2 × 10¹⁴ to 6.6 × 10¹⁵ Hz, a factor of 8. Lyman-α (2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz) lies between. But the line's natural width
is 4 parts in 10⁸, and even Doppler-broadened lines are narrower than 10⁻⁴; an eightfold sweep would spread the light across a factor of 8. A swing that
straight and that eccentric would also radiate a broad comb of harmonics, not one line. That is 4446's first test ("a steady colour while the swing dies";
4446 c), now in numbers for his swing.

So the emission swing cannot be timed by the Coulomb pull on a draining orbit. Whatever times it must hold its rate at ΔE/h while its energy drains, the
way a tuning fork keeps its pitch as it dies away. (In standard physics the charge cloud of an atom caught between two states oscillates at exactly ΔE/h,
however much of each state it holds; the mixture sets the strength, not the rate. That is a fact for CPP to match, not evidence for CPP.) The convenient
branch, "the swing simply runs at ΔE/h", is marked: it is what has to be explained (= 4457 b).

## 8. Answer to his "What do you think?"

Putting the DP displacement along the CP's line of motion, reversing at each turn like the current in an antenna, answers which line it moves on. And I
agree it shouldn't be called ZBW. I'll call it the emission swing, which keeps it apart from CPs passing through each other inside a DP, the distinction he
floated. That also clears up the clash with the free electron's much faster ZBW.

The direction is the hard part. An antenna sends nothing along its own wire; its radiation goes out sideways, all round the wire. So if the photon left
along the CP's swing, it would leave in the one direction the swing doesn't radiate. If it leaves at right angles, the pattern and the polarization fit,
because the displacement is then across the photon's path. But that makes the displacement sideways to the travel, which changes how we've read the July
ruling that it's lengthwise. That's his call. And a kick usually drives a swing along itself, so a kick that sets up a swing across itself still needs
explaining. Also, for single atoms and thin gases, experiments don't show photons following the collision: atoms lit by a laser from one side glow in
every direction, which is why laser cooling works at all.

His half metre matches light speed times hydrogen's 1.6-nanosecond emission time. If that's what sets it, it fits a swing of about four million cycles. A
single oscillation would reach only about a ten-thousandth of a millimetre, and a single cycle can't be one sharp colour.

One more thing to solve. A swing timed by the nucleus's pull would speed up eightfold as it drained, but the colour is far narrower than that allows. So
something must keep the swing's rate fixed while it dies down, the way a tuning fork keeps its pitch as it fades.
