## 3. The swing rate (script part 1)

For hydrogen's 2p → 1s light (2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz), two genuine candidates fail:

- **(b) Either orbit's classical revolution rate,** 6.58 × 10¹⁵ or 8.2 × 10¹⁴ Hz, does not match (nor does their difference). This failure is expected
  for low orbits: only for large orbits does the light's frequency approach the revolution rate (the correspondence principle).

**His "energy difference" is the Bohr condition.** The light's frequency is ΔE/h by definition, so its match to the energy difference is an identity, not
a test. The physical content lies elsewhere. Give each state the rhythm of its whole energy, rest included: (mc² + E_n)/h, about 1.2356 × 10²⁰ Hz for
both orbits. They differ by 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz, the light's frequency. **This answers his "Is the Compton frequency relevant?": yes, as the nearly common
rhythm of both states. The light is the small difference between the two.** It also ties to 4443: each state's rhythm is the rest beat mc²/h, shifted
by its orbital energy.

**What oscillates at the difference.** A plain sum of two rhythms has no part at their difference. Two tuning forks slightly out of tune only make their
loudness pulse; they make no new tone. In standard quantum mechanics the atom's charge, caught between two states, does oscillate at the difference.
That is because the charge goes as the square of the wave, and the square contains a cross term at the difference of the two rhythms. **So in CPP terms,
something that goes as the square of the two rhythms (the CP's density or position, say) must itself swing at ΔE/h.** Then each swing is one cycle of
the light (4445's carrier). This is Claude's reading of his "energy difference", not shown in CPP.

**A steady colour (script part 4).** Lyman-α's linewidth is 10⁸ Hz on 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz, so the swing rate holds constant to about 4 parts in 10⁸ while
the swing dies away. A charge falling in a Coulomb field and changing its rate as it loses energy would spread the colour (a chirp). His falling
picture must keep the rate fixed while the amplitude decays. The square-of-two-rhythms reading passes this test, since its rate is set by the two
levels, not by the swing's size.

## 4. The photon's energy (script part 2, illustrative)

## 5. The rogue-wave trigger (script part 3)

- **It fits the decay law.** Rogue waves arriving at random, at a steady average rate, knock out excited atoms at random times, giving exponential decay,
  the measured law (37%, 13.5% and 5% left after one, two and three lifetimes).
- **It must never knock ground-state atoms up.** In empty space, atoms in the ground state stay there. Random waves must de-excite atoms but never
  excite them, a known difficulty for random-field pictures. (In standard physics spontaneous emission comes from vacuum fluctuations together with the
  atom's own radiation reaction: Dalibard, Dupont-Roc and Cohen-Tannoudji, 1982.)
- **It must feel the surroundings.** Spontaneous decay rates change with the atom's surroundings:
  - enhanced in a resonant cavity (Purcell 1946; observed by Goy, Raimond, Gross and Haroche, 1983);
  - inhibited between mirrors closer than half the wavelength, for the matching polarization (proposed by Kleppner, 1981; observed by Hulet, Hilfer
    and Kleppner, 1985, in a Rydberg atom).

  So the DI-bit sea's rogue waves must be shaped by nearby mirrors and walls.

## 8. Answer to his "What do you think?"

On the direction, I think we agree in outline: the light goes out at right angles to the swing, and the collision picks which way. Two details remain.
Light from atoms spreads over a range of angles, not exactly at right angles, so the collisions must come from a spread of directions. And a kick usually
pushes things along itself, so a kick that sets up a swing at right angles needs explaining. On "forward, not backward": a single photon is always caught
going one way, with the atom kicking back the other way, so nothing has to cancel behind it. If CPP wants the forward direction built up by
reinforcement, that pattern has to be bigger than the light's wavelength, which is a thousand times bigger than the atom. It would live in the DP sea
around the atom.

On the swing rate: yes, the Compton frequency is relevant, though not directly as the light's rate. Each orbit has its own rhythm, set by the electron's
whole energy there, rest energy included. Both rhythms are almost exactly the Compton rate, about 10²⁰ per second, and they differ by just the light's
frequency. That's his "energy difference". The catch is that two rhythms added together don't make a new tone at their difference; two tuning forks
slightly out of tune only make the loudness pulse. Something has to go as the square of the two, as the electron's charge does in standard physics, to
swing at the difference. In CPP terms, something about the CP has to do that.

There's also a test his falling picture has to pass. The light's colour stays sharp, steady to about four parts in a hundred million, while the swing
dies away. A charge falling inward usually speeds up its swing as it loses energy, which would smear the colour.

On energy, we agree: the energy released sets the photon's energy, and since that energy goes with the colour, the share per cycle just follows. His
rogue-wave trigger fits the way atoms decay at random, but it has to pass two more tests. It must never knock atoms in their lowest state upward. And it
must feel nearby mirrors: atoms decay faster in a tuned cavity, and slower between mirrors closer than half the wavelength.
