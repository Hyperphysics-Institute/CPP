## 3. The one-lump puzzle is the one-photon version, and it has been tested directly (script)

The puzzle of 4476 §5 involves one photon, not a pair, but it raises the question Einstein raised at the 1927 Solvay conference: when a particle's wave, spread
over a screen after a pinhole (his example was an electron), is detected at one point, how does the rest of the screen know not to fire? For one photon split to
two detectors it is the same question. Guerreiro, Sanguinetti, Zbinden, Gisin and Suarez (Phys. Lett. A 376, 2174, 2012) did it with detectors about 10 m apart,
timed so that the two clicks were spacelike separated (a light signal between them takes about 33 ns). Coincidences were about 1.2 × 10⁻³ of the rate for
independent detectors in the spacelike arrangement and 1.3 × 10⁻³ in the timelike control: about three orders of magnitude below independence, either way.
(The counts are few, 4 ± 2 per 30 minutes, so each ratio is uncertain by about half, and the residue is near what detector noise alone gives; the conclusion
is not in doubt.)

**A pull at light speed fails this.** In the experiment each click came within about a nanosecond of the photon's arrival, which the herald fixes (1 ns window,
0.4 ns detector jitter), while anything travelling at c from the other detector needs 33 ns. So whenever a detector took the whole energy, nothing from the
other detector could yet have reached it. If a half could fire a detector by itself, the two would fire independently and coincide about 800 times more often
than measured (script). If a half cannot (§4), then neither could fire within the nanosecond, yet each fired at the rate expected for whole photons.
**Parts arriving "at really different times" cannot rescue it,** because the click does not wait. (This takes the commitment to be at absorption, as in his
picture: the Ag bond, the gap.) For scale only: a 1.6 ns Lyman-α train is half a metre long, and a pull 10 m away arrives twenty train-lengths late (script).

**Deciding at the splitter fails only in its simplest form.** If each photon simply took one arm and nothing went the other way, there would be no pull to
explain, but single photons sent through an interferometer that recombines the two arms interfere: each photon's chance of reaching a given output depends on
both arm lengths (Grangier, Roger and Aspect 1986, the second experiment, with cascade photons). That rules out "nothing goes the other way". It does not rule
out the energy taking one arm while a part carrying no energy goes both ways and guides it. That is de Broglie–Bohm, which Guerreiro et al. themselves name as
answering Einstein ("which detector clicks is already determined at the pin-hole"; the other detector "only 'sees' an empty wave"). It matches every
single-photon result here, and with a fixed splitter and no settings Bell's theorem has nothing to say. Its cost in his picture: the photon's energy would not
be spread over the whole cohort (his archive defines it as the total stretch of all its DPs; 4470 f, "one extended excitation"), and the far half would carry
none. CPP already owes a pilot-wave thread (4464 d).

These are standard results, facts any account has to match, not evidence for or against CPP.

## 4. What survives of his picture

The pricked bubble is a good picture of *what* happens: the photon is one extended cohort; an absorber at one place (a silver bond in a film grain, a gap in a
semiconductor) takes all of its energy, and the rest of the cohort is gone from everywhere else. What fails is the *how*, if the rest has to travel there.

Three ways remain, none derived: (i) **gathering that does not travel**, the Nexus holding one photon's cohort as one joint state as it holds a pair; (ii)
**energy committed early**, the energy riding one part from the splitter while an energyless part goes both ways (§3), at the cost of his spread-energy picture;
(iii) **a pull at light speed**, ruled out (§3). On (i): in CPP's own terms the Nexus, which in QM-3 already keeps a pair's joint state whole regardless of
separation by enforcing total DI-bit conservation across all Grid Points at each Absolute Moment, is a candidate that does not travel. If one photon's cohort is
held as one joint state in the same way, then "the rest follows down into the place where the collapse happened" becomes "the conservation is enforced for the
whole cohort at once", with no-signalling (THEO-QM-5) keeping it from carrying a message. That would make the one-lump puzzle the one-particle case of QM-3, and
his "group entity" a name for what the Nexus does. (Claude's reading; convenient, since it reuses machinery already in the record and so looks like progress
without new derivation. QM-3 derives the pair case; the one-photon case, with the energy and its arrival at one absorber, is not derived.) (ii) is the less
convenient branch for this lane, since it would move the energy off the cohort, and it is kept open, not dismissed.

A smaller point: energy reaching one absorber all at once is also what the absorber needs. A photon at 2 eV is absorbed across a 1.1 eV silicon gap; half of it,
1 eV, would not be. So "arriving at really different times" at one absorber also needs the absorber to hold the early part until the rest arrives, which a
single electron crossing a gap does not obviously do. The same holds for the detector in the experiment: an InGaAs gap (about 0.75 eV) absorbs the whole 0.80 eV
photon at 1550 nm but not a 0.40 eV half. That is why §3 argues from the click time and not from the half acting alone. (Claude's reading.)

## 7. Reply to his "What do you think?"

You're right that it's the same family as Aspect's problem; it's the one-photon version, the one Einstein raised in 1927. But the record has two layers. The early website versions and a passage in the superdeterminism paper did describe entanglement as
local, built from light-speed steps, and that is probably what you remember. The reviewed entanglement paper (QM-3) dropped its first version in so many words
("The previous CPP argument was wrong"), and its reviewed position is that entanglement comes from the Nexus, which keeps the pair's joint state whole at every
moment without any signal travelling between the two sites. On the record's own numbers, the local readings can't produce the measured correlations.

And for one photon a light-speed pull has been tested directly. In Geneva in 2012, a single photon was split to two detectors ten metres apart, timed so that no
light signal could get from one click to the other. They still almost never both fired: about a thousand times less often than two independent detectors would. Each detector fires within
about a nanosecond of the photon arriving, but a pull travelling at light speed needs 33 ns to cross, so it can't do the job, however the parts are spread in
time. The photon can't simply take one path at the mirror with nothing going the other way, because the same photons interfere through both paths. One option
is still open, though: the energy takes one path while a part with no energy goes both ways and steers it. That's the pilot-wave picture. It would mean the
energy isn't spread over the whole cohort, which cuts against your picture.

Your pricked bubble is still a good picture of what happens. What has to change is how the rest "follows down": not by travelling, but by something that holds
the whole cohort as one, which is what the Nexus already does for an entangled pair. That sounds a lot like your group entity (in the archive you made it one per
quantum and faster than light; the Nexus is one for everything). It's convenient for me to say
so, since it reuses what we have, and I've marked it; the one-photon case isn't derived.
