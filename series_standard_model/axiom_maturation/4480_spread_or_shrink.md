## 3. Shrink or spread: what blurs a glowing molecule's fringes (script)

He gives a mechanism for 4479 §5: each emitted photon takes ħ units away, so the molecules arrive having lost more or less, and comparing unequal molecules
blurs the fringes. Two checks against the C70 experiments (Hackermüller et al. 2004; Hornberger et al. 2003):
- **The size of the loss.** One thermal photon of about 1 eV changes a C70 molecule's mass, and so its de Broglie wavelength, by about 1.3 × 10⁻¹²; a hundred
  photons by about 10⁻¹⁰ (script, (1)). The selected beam already spreads in speed by 8–35% (full width; Brezger et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 88, 100404, 2002, the same apparatus). A
  near-field interferometer is sensitive to speed, through the Talbot length d²/λ_dB, but its visibility changes over tens of percent in speed. A change about
  10¹¹ times smaller than the spread it already survives cannot blur the fringes by that route.
- **What the loss depends on.** The measured fading depends on the emitted light's wavelength against the separation of the two paths: light much longer than
  the separation leaves the fringes, light shorter than it erases them (standard decoherence factor, sinc(2π Δx/λ): for a 1 μm separation, 0.05 at 0.7 μm, 0.41
  at 3 μm, 0.94 at 10 μm; script, (2)). It also depends on where along the interferometer the light is emitted: one emission "is sufficient to determine the path
  of the molecule if the emission occurs close to the second grating" (Hackermüller et al. 2004). A loss of mass-energy alone would act the same wherever and at whatever wavelength it happened. Gas collisions (Hornberger et al. 2003) blur fringes with no emission at all; in his terms they add or remove groups and give kicks, so this case
  does not separate the readings either.
- **Read as momentum, the loss is the right size.** Each photon also carries off momentum h/λ. For a 1 eV photon (1.24 μm) the sideways kick to C70 is
  3.8 × 10⁻⁴ m/s, about 0.8 of the kick the grating itself gives (h/md = 4.8 × 10⁻⁴ m/s), while the forward change is 2 × 10⁻⁶ of the speed (script, (3)). A
  random sideways kick of that size, averaged over directions, gives the same sinc(2π Δx/λ) and the same dependence on place. In standard physics the recoil kick
  and the which-way record are two descriptions of one effect (Pfau, Spälter, Kurtsiefer, Ekstrom and Mlynek, Phys. Rev. Lett. 73, 1223, 1994; Chapman et al.,
  Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 3783, 1995, where the lost coherence was recovered by keeping only atoms correlated with photons sent into a limited range of
  directions). So if the ħ units he says are lost carry momentum, his shrinkage gets the size and the dependence right, and the C70 data cannot tell it from
  spreading. (Claude's reading of "quantum elements" as including momentum.)

So what blurs the fringes is not that the molecule became lighter. It is the light it gave off: by its kick or, equivalently, by the record it carries of which
way the molecule went. For the C70 experiments the two descriptions agree. What separates them is marking without a kick. In quantum-eraser experiments the
path is marked in polarization, which gives no kick. Then the which-way record held in the polarizations of the photon and its partner together is read in a
way that cannot tell the paths apart, and the fringes come back as fringes and antifringes sorted by the partner's result (Walborn, Terra Cunha, Pádua and
Monken, Phys. Rev. A 65, 033818, 2002, with photons, including a delayed version). For atoms, path marking whose push on the atom is too small to explain the
loss still wipes the fringes out (Dürr, Nonn and Rempe, Nature 395, 33, 1998). Nothing was added back to the photon that went through the slits; only how its
partner was read changed. A lost kick cannot explain that; a record still joined to the group can. In group terms (Claude's reading), the emitted light stays
joined to the molecule's group and carries the record. (No such eraser has been done for light from heated molecules; the photon case is the direct one.)

So his "smaller quantum group" is right as energy bookkeeping, and right as a description of the C70 fading if the lost units carry momentum. "The group
spreads" is what the no-kick erasers require, and the two can both hold: the group
now includes the light. These are standard results, facts for CPP to match.

## 4. What his pair answer implies

If "collapsed" means "combines with a larger group", then when one photon of a pair is absorbed by an atom, the partner, still in flight, becomes predictable
from the atom's record: perfectly correlated for matched settings, Bell-correlated for others. Two readings fit. (i) The partner is now joined to the atom's
enlarged group, though it never touched it. This is 4478 §3's merging applied at a distance, read without collapse, and it is the branch convenient to §3's
"spread". (ii) The pair's group ends, and the partner goes on as a quantum of its own, in a definite state correlated with the record. This is the collapse
reading. Both give the same statistics and no signalling. His words ("a single quantum until it is collapsed and combines with a larger group") do not say
whether the whole pair joins or only the absorbed photon. (Claude's reading; what the Nexus has to keep either way is the correlation.)

## 7. Reply to his "What do you think?"

I agree on the unit and on the pair. One ħ unit as a DP's ZBW, and a quantum as a group of those units, fits what the record says about the ZBW, and it answers
my question: an entangled pair holds more than one unit but is still one quantum, until it combines with something bigger, an orbital or a band gap, which is
your collapse. Two things to settle later: the factor between ħ and h, and the electron's spin, which is half your unit, and your own earlier ruling that ħ/2 is the smallest step.

On the glowing molecule: if the shrinking is the energy, the mass the molecule loses, it's far too small. One photon of heat light changes a fullerene's mass
by about a part in a million million, and the beam's own speeds already differ by ten to thirty percent. But each photon also carries off a push, and that push
is big enough: sideways, it's about as large as the kick the grating itself gives. Read that way, your "losing quantum elements" is a standard way of
describing the same fading, and it gets the details right. Long-wave light gives too small a push to matter, short-wave light a big one, and the push spoils
the fringes most where the two paths are farthest apart. Where the pictures may part is what happens to the light afterwards. With entangled photons, the path
can be marked with no push at all, and reading the partner light a certain way brings the fringes back. That says the light carrying the record stays tied to
the group. So I'd put it this way: the molecule does get smaller and does get pushed, and its group also takes in the light. That's why the record can matter.
