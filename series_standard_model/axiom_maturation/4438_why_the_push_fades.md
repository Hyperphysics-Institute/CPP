## 2. Why the push would be less (script parts 1–2)

**In relativity, it need not be.** Gravity acts on energy. GR fixes only a body's total motion. At first order (isotropic coordinates), toward the
mass that is 1 + v² times g when moving sideways and 1 − 3v² times g when moving straight toward or away (script part 1). How the total is split
into a push and a turning is a bookkeeping. Two bookkeepings on file both match GR:
- **4434's:** the push gives the same acceleration g at every speed (a force growing with the energy), and a moving CP turns at half light's rate.
- **4436's:** the push fades with speed, and a moving CP turns at light's full rate.

In both, light is not pushed: its bending is all refraction (4434 §3, 4435). So his question is fair. Nothing in relativity alone makes the push
weaker. If the push fades in CPP, it is because of how a CP turns.

**What decides it: how fast a moving CP turns.**
1. **If a moving CP turns at light's full rate.** By the 4415 rule a CP steps at a fraction f of the local light speed c/K. If its direction turns
   toward the slower side at the Snell rate, as light's does, it turns by 2v²g sideways, and along its path its speed follows c/K. That full-rate
   turning is 4436's quantification, an assumption still to be shown in CPP's mechanics.
2. **Light turns fully, with no push.** "No push" is his own 4437 statement ("light, with no rest mass, only turns"). "Fully" (2g sideways, twice
   Newton's) rests on the WC-DP-SEA-PV convention K = 1 + 2U (4392), which puts in relativity's value for how much space slows light (4436 lists it as
   an assumption).
3. **Then the push must fade.** Given full-rate turning, the push weight that matches GR in both directions is exactly 1 − f²: the push changes f by
   (1 − f²)g, sideways and along alike (script part 2: 1 + v² and 1 − 3v²). With an unfaded push and full turning, a CP near light speed would bend by
   3g sideways, half again more than light.

**The case for this split over 4434's.** Both give the right totals, and both join light's totals smoothly (1 + v² goes to 2 at light speed). They
differ in the parts. In 4434's split, a CP near light speed would still turn at half light's rate, and still feel the full push, while light at the
same speed turns fully and feels none. In 4436's split, both parts join light's: the turning reaches light's and the push fades to zero. If a CP's
turning works the way light's does (the 4415 rule ties its step to the local light speed), 4436's split is the natural one. This is a reasonable
argument, not a proof. It rests on the full-rate turning and the K convention.

**In register terms (Claude's candidate).** Under 4425's reading (SSV_abs ↔ energy, |SSV_net| ↔ momentum, with its mass-shell condition as an input
and its fidelity conditions still owed), f = |SSV_net|/SSV_abs, and

  1 − f² = (SSV_abs² − SSV_net²) / SSV_abs² = (m/E)².

The square root of the numerator, √(SSV_abs² − SSV_net²), is the register's **rest part**: what is left when the motion is taken out, the CPP
counterpart of rest mass (4437). So the rule would read: **the limb push acts on the CP in proportion to the square of its register's rest share.**
That fits his 4437 statement that **gravity's push works on the rest mass**. A further reading, that the part of the register tied up in the motion
does not feel the push, is Claude's extension of his "no radial force connection" between the motion's arc-separation and the rest mass. Why the rest
share enters squared, and not to the first power, is not derived.

**A candidate that fails.** Claude first tried a "clock" reading: a moving CP's organization runs slow by γ (4424), so it would receive the push 1/γ
as often, a force (m/γ)g. With 4424's inertia sideways (γm) that gives g/γ², which matches; the match is a coincidence of the two factors. Along the
path, the inertia is γ³m, so the push gives g/γ⁴ and the total is 1 − 4v² + v⁴, not 1 − 3v² (script part 2). It is dropped. The push must act on the
step fraction f directly, as in 4436's step rule, not as a force on the inertia.

## 3. The two balls (script part 3)

**The answer is 1.25 times the pair's rest energy, not 2.5 times; and it is not contested.** As he worded it ("2.5 times the mass energy of the
original two balls"), the number is 1.25. The 2.5 in 4437 was in units of one ball's mass. Each ball of mass m at 0.6c carries γmc² = 1.25 mc², so the
pair carries 2.5 mc², against their rest energy 2 mc².

**What the recorder captures.** The balls' 2 mc² stays as their matter. The recorder takes in the 0.5 mc² of kinetic energy as heat. Together, the
mass of recorder plus balls rises by 2.5 m over the recorder's own, of which 0.5 m is the captured energy, the energy spent accelerating them. This is
energy conservation with E = γmc², measured routinely in accelerator calorimeters.

The 4437 question ("one rest mass or two") was a matter of definition and is withdrawn. Relativity calls the pair's total energy in its own rest frame
(here the lab) its invariant mass, 2.5 m. That such energy weighs is tested inside matter: the internal kinetic and binding energies of nuclei fall
with everything else (equivalence-principle tests). For the free pair as a source of gravity, the statement is that its total energy gravitates; that
is not tested directly.

**At first order GR's motion is two numbers**, sideways and along. **CPP has a split that matches both**, built from CPP pieces. It rests on three
assumptions:
- the K convention, K = 1 + 2U (4392);
- the full Snell-rate turning of a moving CP (4436);
- the push weighted by the square of the register's rest share (§2).

| Toward the mass, / g (first order) | Push: changes f by (1 − f²)g | Light-speed part: Snell-rate turning, speed following c/K | Total (GR) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slow mass | 1 (all of its fall) | ≈ 0 | 1 |
| Fast mass, sideways | 1 − v² | 2v² | 1 + v² |

## 7. Answer to his "Any ideas?" and "Do we understand?"

His question is a good one. In relativity itself nothing says the push has to weaken. Gravity acts on energy, and relativity fixes only the total
bending, not how it divides between a push and a turning. There are two ways to divide it on file. In one, the push stays the same at every speed and
a moving particle turns at half light's rate. In the other, a moving particle turns just as fully as light does, and the push fades with speed. The
second is the natural one for CPP if a CP's turning works like light's, since the 4415 rule ties its step to the local light speed. Then, as the
particle nears light speed, its push has to fade to nothing so that it bends exactly like light, which gets no push at all, as he said. The fading
that matches relativity in every direction is the square of the share of the particle's register that is not tied up in its motion. That fits his
rule that gravity's push works on the rest mass. Why it is the square is still to be shown, and so is the full-rate turning.

On the two balls, it isn't contested, but the number is 1.25 times the pair's resting energy, not 2.5 times. My 2.5 counted one ball's mass as the
unit: the pair carries 2.5 of those units of energy, against the 2 they started with. The recorder takes in the extra half unit as heat. My "one mass
or two" question was only about a definition, and I withdraw it.

On whether we understand it: at first order, all of relativity's motion is two numbers, sideways and along. CPP's split gives both, with his two
bullets as its rules, provided three things hold: how much space slows light near a mass, a moving CP turning as fully as light, and the push acting
on the resting share. Those need to be shown in CPP's own mechanics, and then come the second-order effects, such as the slow swing of Mercury's
orbit. As far as we can see now, it's a finite list, not an endless hole.
