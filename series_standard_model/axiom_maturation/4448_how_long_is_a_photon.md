## 2. A one-cycle photon makes two predictions, and both fail (script parts 1–2)

His proposal is clear and testable: one photon lasts one wavelength's time, 1/f, so a higher-energy photon is shorter.

- **Its colour would be smeared.** A pulse N cycles long spreads its frequencies over about 1/N of its central frequency. A one-cycle pulse spreads
  over about a third to a half of its colour in all, depending on how the width is defined (script part 1). Hydrogen's red Hα line (656 nm), as
  one-cycle photons, would run from yellow-green into the infrared (about 566 to 780 nm, or 540 to 840 nm). Measured atomic
  lines are sharp. Hydrogen's Lyman-α has a natural width of about 4 parts in 10⁸ of its frequency. That sharpness means the photon lasts about 4
  million cycles (4445).
- **Its fringes would vanish beyond one wavelength.** Send single photons one at a time into an interferometer that splits each and recombines the
  halves after paths of different length. Fringes appear only while the path difference is shorter than the photon's length (Grangier, Roger and Aspect
  showed single-photon interference in 1986). A one-cycle photon would lose its fringes once the arms differed by more than one wavelength, about 0.1 to
  0.8 μm. By first-order coherence, a single photon's fringe reach equals the coherence length set by its line width: about 0.5 m for hydrogen's
  natural Lyman-α packet. Photons from single atoms or ions in cavities have packets of hundreds of nanoseconds to about a microsecond (Kuhn, Hennrich
  and Rempe 2002; Keller and others 2004), which implies a reach of tens to hundreds of metres; that reach is inferred from the packet length, not
  measured directly (script part 2).

**Two faces of one property.** The fringe visibility against path difference is the Fourier transform of the spectrum (the Wiener–Khinchin
theorem), so the line's sharpness and the fringes' reach are one fact seen two ways. Both say a photon from an atom lasts millions of cycles. The colour
cannot be the duration of a one-cycle event. It must be the steady rate of an oscillation that runs through the whole packet.

**What his intuition gets right.**
- **One-cycle light exists.** Ultrafast lasers make pulses only a few cycles long, even single-cycle and attosecond pulses. They are exactly as broad
  in colour as the bandwidth argument says, spanning about an octave. So a one-cycle photon is physically possible; it is just not a sharp-line atomic
  photon.
- **Higher-energy atomic photons do tend to be shorter.** The spontaneous emission rate grows as the cube of the frequency times the square of the
  transition strength, so higher-frequency lines tend to have shorter lifetimes (Lyman-α 1.6 ns, against tens of ns for Balmer lines). They are still
  millions of cycles long.
- **A higher-energy photon delivers more energy,** hf. The energy rises with the rate of the oscillation, not because the packet is shorter. Two photons
  of the same colour carry the same energy whatever their packet lengths (4446, 4447). What "amplitude" means for a single photon (its energy hf spread
  over its packet's field) is to be settled.

## 3. The beat between two photons, and the ZBW's 10²⁰ Hz (script part 3)

**Two light sources do beat.** Shine two lasers together on a fast detector and it ticks at the difference of their frequencies. This was first seen
with ordinary light (Forrester, Gudmundsen and Johnson, 1955). The beat is at the difference, f₁ − f₂, not at any ZBW rate. And it holds steady over
many beat periods only if each source's oscillation is steady over many cycles: a steady beat over a millisecond at 500 nm needs each oscillation steady
over about 6 × 10¹¹ cycles. (The 1955 beat was very noisy because its sources had short coherence, which illustrates the same point.) So the beat itself shows that light carries a steady oscillation at its own frequency.

**The ZBW's rate.** The corpus gives two rates for CPP's ZBW (4443): a Planck-scale rate (about 1/(2t_P)) in one record, and a Compton-scale rate (about
1.2 × 10²⁰ Hz for the electron) in another. Visible and ultraviolet light runs at 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ Hz, a hundred thousand to a million times slower than the
Compton rate. So a photon's oscillation is not the free electron's ZBW, as he suspected. If the ZBW is the carrier, as he proposes, then something must
make the radiated field oscillate at the much slower f = E/h. Either the ZBW is modulated at that rate (4447 §4; he set the orbits' beat aside), or the
CP's swing in the atom during the decay runs at that rate rather than at the free electron's ZBW rate (4445). His sentence, "the distinction may be
that the ZBW oscillation rate equals the CP rate. But I thought … 10²⁰", has two readings; the "but" shows his CP rate is not 10²⁰ Hz:
- **(i)** the CP's swing while emitting runs at the photon's rate, not at 10²⁰ Hz;
- **(ii)** the CP's Moment or swap rate (Planck-scale, matching the 1/(2t_P) record in 4443).

Both are Claude's readings, and §5 asks. The conclusion that the free electron's ZBW is not the photon's rate holds even more strongly for a
Planck-scale rate.

## 6. Answer to his "What do you think?"

The one-wavelength photon is a clear proposal, and it can be tested two ways. Both say no.
- **The colour.** A pulse only one cycle long would contain a wide smear of colours, a third to a half of its own colour in all: hydrogen's red line
  would run from yellow-green into the infrared. Atomic lines are sharp to a few parts in a hundred million, which means each photon lasts millions of
  cycles.
- **The fringes.** A photon one wavelength long, split and recombined, would stop making fringes once one path was longer than the other by more than
  a wavelength, under a micrometre. A photon as sharp in colour as an atom's should keep its fringes over tens of centimetres or more. (These are the
  same fact seen two ways.)

So the colour has to be the steady rate of an oscillation that runs through the whole packet, not the length of a single event. Parts of his idea
are right. One-cycle light does exist: lasers can make pulses a single cycle long, and they are smeared across a huge range of colours, exactly as the
argument says. They just aren't atomic photons. And higher-energy atomic photons do tend to come in shorter packets, though still millions of cycles
long. Higher-energy photons deliver more energy, but that goes with a faster oscillation, not a shorter packet.

On the beat: yes, two light sources beat, at the difference of their frequencies. For the beat to stay steady, each source's light has to keep a steady
oscillation over a huge number of cycles, so the beat itself shows the oscillation is there. And yes, he's right that the ZBW rate of a free electron,
about 10²⁰ per second, is far too fast to be visible light, which runs at about 10¹⁵. So if the ZBW is the mechanism, something has to slow the radiated
oscillation to the photon's rate. His "the ZBW rate equals the CP rate" could mean that: while the atom emits, the CP's swing might run at the photon's rate
rather than the free electron's. Or he may mean the rate at which CPs swap each Moment. I've asked which, along with what repeats through the packet.
