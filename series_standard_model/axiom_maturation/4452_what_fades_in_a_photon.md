## 1. His question

When the photon's edges rise and fall smoothly, or the whole photon drops smoothly, **what is dropping: the frequency or the amplitude? And is it a single
photon, or a beam of many photons?**

## 2. The answer

**The amplitude drops; the frequency does not.** What fades is the strength of the oscillation, its envelope. The rate of the oscillation, the colour,
stays the same from start to finish. For Lyman-α the line's natural width is about 4 parts in 10⁸ of its frequency. In his picture that would be the
swing's radiated strength tapering while its rate holds.

**The start is sharp; only the fall is smooth.** 4451 described only the fall as smooth; to be explicit about the start, it is not gradual. For an atom
excited suddenly, the emission starts at full strength (within the few-nanosecond excitation pulse, in practice) and then fades exponentially, losing the
same share of what is left in each stretch of time.

**Amplitude and energy.** The clicks below trace the strength squared, the energy. The swing's amplitude fades at half that rate.

**It is measured on single photons, but only through many repeats** (script). In single-atom experiments, one atom is excited by a short pulse and
emits one photon, which the detector records as one click at one moment (Darquié and others, 2005: one rubidium atom held in an optical tweezer,
lifetime about 26 ns). One click shows no shape. Repeat it thousands of times and note when each click came after the excitation: the clicks pile up in
an exponential, 63% in the first lifetime, 23% in the second, 9% in the third. That histogram is the envelope (in energy) of the photons, built up the
way the double-slit fringes build up dot by dot (4444). A beam of many atoms gives the same shape.

**What the histogram alone cannot tell.** Packets of other shapes and random lengths can add up to the same exponential histogram: flat packets of
suitably spread lengths do. What separates "each photon is one fading packet" from "packets of varying shape that only add up to the fading curve" is
making two single photons interfere. Identical fading packets overlap fully and interfere well; packets of varying shape overlap only partly. Beugnon
and others (2006) made photons from two separately trapped rubidium atoms interfere; the result is consistent with identical exponential packets.
Later experiments reach near-perfect two-photon interference: single trapped ions (Maunz and others, 2007), and quantum dots, which are not atoms,
above 95% (Somaschi and others, 2016).

**The interpretation.** In standard quantum mechanics a single photon's envelope is a probability amplitude, never seen in one shot. Read literally, CPP
would have each photon be a fading swing. That literal reading predicts near-perfect interference between photons from identical atoms: a consistency
check for CPP, and a test.

**What this means for his picture.** Each photon's swing would start at full strength when the CP is knocked loose and fade smoothly as the reservoir
drains, at a fixed rate. The question in 4451 stands: as the reservoir drains, what happens to its push on the CP?

## 4. Answer to his question

It's the amplitude that drops, not the frequency. The light's colour, its rate of oscillation, stays the same from start to finish; what fades is how
strong the oscillation is. To be clear about the start: it isn't gradual. An atom that has just been knocked loose begins emitting at full strength, and
then the strength fades away, losing the same share of what's left in each stretch of time.

And it's about single photons, though you only see the shape by repeating the experiment. One atom, excited once, gives one photon, and the detector
records it as a single click at one moment. A single click shows no shape. But excite the same atom thousands of times and note how long after each
excitation the click came: the clicks pile up into that fading curve. It builds up the same way the double-slit pattern builds up one dot at a time.

That pile-up alone could also come from photons of varying shapes. The sharper test is to make two single photons from two separate atoms interfere.
They interfere well only if each has the same fading shape, and experiments find that they do. If CPP's photon really is one fading swing, that is what it
should predict.
