- **The slit pattern's width goes with the colour**, violet narrow and red wide, as measured. The order matches; his reason, the packet's shortness, is not

- **His particle picture resembles the de Broglie–Bohm pilot wave.** In that interpretation (de Broglie 1927; Bohm 1952) each particle goes through one slit,

## 3. What fails: one rise and fall is one cycle (script parts 1 and 3)

**The colour spreads.** A single push and pull is one cycle of light. Choosing its length so that its colour peaks at Lyman-α, the rise and fall lasts
3.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ s. Its colour then spreads over a band about as wide as its own frequency (half-power width 1.07 times the peak), against Lyman-α's natural width
of 4 parts in 10⁸. That is 4450's result: one cycle cannot be one colour. A grating sends a one-cycle pulse into a rainbow, not a line.

**It is short.** The pulse is light speed × duration long: about 0.10 μm for Lyman-α, about 0.5 μm for the sodium D line. On his wording here, the envelope
runs "leading edge/front to middle, to trailing edge" with "an axial distribution along the axis of travel", so these are lengths along the travel, and the
photon would be a tenth of a micrometre long, not "the 1 meter volume" his slit explanation uses. (If his metre is sideways, as in 4460, this conflict does
not arise; the other three failures stand. Marked, §9.)

**It interferes with itself over about one wavelength of path difference.** How many fringes a light can show, as the path difference grows, is about its
frequency ÷ its spread of frequencies: about 1 for this pulse. White light shows only a few coloured fringes near the centre of a double-slit pattern; with
the same slits, laser light shows fringes as far out as the slits' own diffraction allows, and in a Michelson interferometer laser fringes persist over path
differences of metres. Single photons from one emitter, an atom or a quantum dot, sent through a Michelson interferometer show fringes whose visibility
falls off only as the path difference approaches light speed × the emitter's coherence time: centimetres to tens of centimetres for nanosecond lifetimes. A
photon a tenth of a micrometre long could not overlap with itself delayed by a centimetre.

**It carries far too little energy.** By the standard radiation rule (Larmor), an electron that speeds up and slows down once over a few ångströms, with a
top speed of about 1.9 × 10⁶ m/s (its speed if all 10.2 eV were its kinetic energy), radiates about 2 × 10⁻⁶ eV. The photon carries 10.2 eV, about five
million times more. That factor is of the same order as the four million cycles in the lifetime (4461 §3, check 1); the classical radiation-damping estimate
gives the same order. The factor scales as 1 ÷ the top speed squared, and the top speed is a constructed value. CPP's radiation rule is owed (§6 i), so this
is a check against the standard rule, marked (§9). He agreed in 4457 that a harder-accelerated charge radiates more, the same rule in outline.

**So, by these four measurements, the colour cannot be one rise and fall.** What works in §2 would survive if the rise and fall were the envelope and the
colour a swing repeating inside it, millions of times, the CP reversing at ΔE/h (4459–4461). That returns to the picture he set aside; Claude records it as
Claude's reading, not as his view (§4, §9).

## 7. The experiment he asked about

It is one of the oldest and best-checked rules in optics, measured many ways:
- **Young's two-slit fringes** (1803): he measured the fringe spacing and found it differs by colour, wider for red than for violet. **Fresnel's diffraction**
  (1818) worked out the spreading behind slits and edges.
- **Laser beams.** Measuring a beam's width with a camera or a knife edge at several distances shows it spreading at a half-angle of λ ÷ (π × w₀), where w₀
  is the starting radius (Gaussian-beam theory; Kogelnik and Li, 1966). Beams of different colours with the same starting width spread in proportion to their
  wavelengths.
- **Telescopes and microscopes.** The smallest detail an instrument can resolve is about 1.22 × wavelength ÷ aperture (the Rayleigh limit), because light
  spreads by that angle on passing through the aperture. That is why radio telescopes are huge, and why microscopes resolve finer detail in blue light or
  ultraviolet than in red.
