## 3. Three checks (script parts 1, 2 and 4)

**1. How much can leave per pass.** Lyman-α carries 10.2 eV and is emitted over the 1.6 ns lifetime, about 3.9 million cycles. So on average only about one
part in four million of the interval leaves per cycle: about 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ eV in the first cycle (as in 4449 §2, 4451 §2; the new part is applying it to his
recharge). If each pass radiated the whole high-to-low difference, the interval would be spent in one pass. The photon would then be one cycle long, with a
band about 0.8 times its own frequency (4460 §4, 4450), against a measured quality factor (2π × frequency × lifetime) of 2.5 × 10⁷. Only if such passes went
on for the measured lifetime would it come to about four million photons' worth.

His words admit three readings:
- **His "picked up … a quantum … radiated it out"**, read most directly: the whole picked-up quantum is radiated each pass. If that quantum is the full
  interval, the photon is one cycle long, which fails 4450.
- **His "quantum" is itself the small per-pass share**, about one four-millionth of the interval. That fits the budget with no return to the pool.
- **The drop is large but most of it goes back into the pool at the recharge**, and the light is the small leak per pass, as in an antenna or a tuning fork,
  where the energy sloshes back and forth and a small share leaves each cycle. This is Claude's reading, and the convenient one (§9).

§6 asks which he means.

**2. The same amount each pass, or the same share of what is left (4451 §3).** If each pass radiates the same amount until the pool is empty, the photon
has a flat envelope that stops abruptly, and its line has side lobes: in the script, 4.6% of the peak between 1.2 and 1.8 lifetimes⁻¹ away (4451: 0.047).
If each pass radiates the same share of what is left, the envelope fades exponentially and the line has no side lobes (0.4% there, falling smoothly). The
measured decay is exponential (4451, 4452). So the leak per pass must fall as the pool drains. But a dipole radiates according to how big its swing is
(the Larmor rule; 4451 §2's radiation-damping argument), so a full-size pass would radiate the same every time. **For the light to fade, the swing itself
must shrink as the pool drains**, and then the turning points do not stay fixed.

**3. The rate of a pass between the two levels, timed by the Coulomb pull** (script part 4; cf. 4449, 4460 §5 for Kepler timing of a swing through the
nucleus and the eightfold sweep, and 4451 §3 for an upper-turning swing from n = 3 having one rate). It is a rough model:
- the CP falls from rest at the upper Bohr radius, through the nucleus, to rest at the lower Bohr radius;
- the energy difference is taken out at the nucleus, where the point-Coulomb speed diverges and the time spent is negligible (under a conservative pull
  alone it would come back out to the upper radius);
- the recharge leg, from the low radius back up to the high level, is given zero time;
- the turning points are the Bohr radii of circular orbits. A zero-angular-momentum orbit of the level's energy turns at 2n²a₀, which would lower every
  rate by 2^(3/2) ≈ 2.8.
- This model is pass-through. 4451 used the bounce convention (2.33 × 10¹⁵ Hz turning at 4a₀), so the numbers are not directly comparable.

| Line | Pass rate | Measured | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lyman-α (2 → 1) | 2.1 × 10¹⁵ Hz | 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz | 0.84 |
| Lyman-β (3 → 1) | 6.7 × 10¹⁴ Hz | 2.92 × 10¹⁵ Hz | 0.23 |
| Balmer-α (3 → 2) | 5.3 × 10¹⁴ Hz | 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz | 1.16 |

The absolute ratios depend on the choice of turning points and carry nothing. The ordering does. A higher starting level makes the pass longer and so
slower, but the light from it is bluer. Lyman-β is measured above Lyman-α, at 1.19 times its rate. The pass puts Lyman-β at about a third of Lyman-α's
pass rate, and about a quarter of its own measured rate. And Lyman-β against Balmer-α, measured at 6.4 to one, comes out at 1.25 to one. A finite recharge
leg adds time that grows with the upper level, which makes the order worse. So a point CP timed by the nucleus's pull, even turning at both levels, does
not give hydrogen's lines. That is 4449's and 4451's result again, now with both endpoints in.

## 4. A candidate within his postulate (Claude's, marked)

What the checks need is a swing whose rate stays fixed while it shrinks, and is set by both levels. A restoring force in proportion to the displacement does
the first: the rate then does not depend on the size of the swing (the mass on a spring of 4451). Inside a uniform sphere of charge +e, opposite in sign to
the CP, the pull back is in proportion to the displacement, and the rate equals Lyman-α's for a sphere of radius about 1.9 Bohr radii (script part 3). A CP
displaced inside a cloud of its own sign would be pushed out. So the candidate needs the extended unit to present net opposite charge to a displaced CP,
for example DP polarization with its positive poles turned toward the electron CP. Whether it does is open (§7 d, i). **If the cloud around the CP pulls it
back in proportion to how far it is displaced**, its swing keeps a fixed rate as it dies away.

That is Thomson's 1904 atom in outline, and it shares Thomson's failure: one cloud gives one rate, not a family of lines set by two levels. In standard
physics the rate comes from both states at once: each has its own rhythm, (mc² + E_n)/h (4446), and the charge centre of an atom caught between two states
swings at the difference. He set aside a beat between orbits in 4448 ("No, not beats between orbits"). So what in his extended unit fixes the rate by both
levels is open; the candidate is offered for the shrink-at-a-fixed-rate part only.

## 5. The sideways step

Each pass ends with a small sideways thrust. What the steps do to the swing's axis decides the light:
- **Steps that cancel:** a fixed axis, linear polarization (as in the m = 0 transition).
- **A steady slow turn at rate Ω:** the plane of polarization rotates, which splits the line by about Ω/2π. To stay inside Lyman-α's natural width
  (1.0 × 10⁸ Hz), the total turn over a lifetime must be under about a radian, about 10⁻⁷ rad per pass.
- **A random wander:** depolarized light, and a broadened line.
- **Circular light** (m = ±1, circular when viewed along the axis, linear edge-on) needs the charge to go round once per optical cycle, which a pass through
  the nucleus with small sideways steps does not do.

So the sideways steps must nearly cancel. How much each step turns the axis is owed.

## 8. Answer to his "What do you think?"

The extended unit, the CP as a combination of clouds, goes the way the Schrödinger picture does: there the electron's charge is spread out, and when an atom
is caught between two states, the centre of that spread swings back and forth at the colour's rate. And if the nucleus's pull overrides the kick, as he
thinks probable, that would solve the problem I raised. The swing would just run through the nucleus along whatever line the CP is on, so nothing has to
swing across the kick. That also fits atoms glowing in every direction, though it doesn't fit his earlier idea that the photon follows the collision, so
that's still open.

What's new in his sequence is that each pass runs from the high level on one side to the low level on the other, so both levels count, which we found they
must. And as in 4451, if the turning points stayed put, the swing would keep one rate.

Three checks, though. First, a photon is emitted over about four million cycles, so on average each pass can only let out about one four-millionth of the
energy. If one pass let out the whole difference, the photon would be one cycle long, and a single cycle can't be one sharp colour. So either his
"quantum" per pass is that tiny share, or most of each drop goes back into the pool, the way a tuning fork's energy sloshes back and forth and only a
little leaves each swing. Second, the light is measured fading away smoothly, not shutting off, so the leak has to get smaller as the pool empties, which
means the swing itself has to shrink. Third, if the nucleus's pull times each pass, a higher starting level gives a slower pass, but the light from it is
bluer, so that can't be what sets the colour. And the small sideways steps must nearly cancel, or the colour would split.

What would fit is a swing that keeps the same rate as it shrinks, like a weight on a spring. If the cloud around the CP pulls it back harder the farther it
is pulled off centre, it does exactly that, but that needs the cloud to attract the CP. That part is my suggestion, and it still has to explain why both
levels set the rate.

So I've asked how much of each drop leaves as light and where the rest goes, and whether the CP keeps turning at the same places as the pool empties.
