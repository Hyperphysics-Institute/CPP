## 2. What it answers

4474 §5 asked how the arcs come into line and whether the CP's pull still turns them once the rogue wave has disturbed it. His answer moves the question: the
driving force is the energy difference between the two orbitals. The CP's give-and-take is not gone: after the drop it sustains the lower orbital's share,
which, in his words, is "somewhat like that amount of DP-arc polarization dropping down and being sustained". What it no longer sustains is the excess, and that
is shed. Whether the arcs swing, ease in or turn steadily is not answered. (Claude's reading.)

Two things follow:
- **The photon's energy is fixed by bookkeeping, if each orbital's energy is the whole: cloud plus the CP's motion.** The cloud holds the upper orbital's energy;
  the lower orbital keeps its share; what is shed totals the difference, ΔE, whatever the details of the shedding. The pieces need not be tuned to add up
  (4472 §5, 4473 §4). Two cautions. First, the electron moves faster in the lower orbital (for n = 2 → 1 its motion gains 10.2 eV while the electrical energy
  falls by 20.4 eV), and he says the arcs' discharge drives that motion. So "the cloud has the same energy after the drop" must leave room for the CP's gain, or
  the shed amount is not ΔE. Second, the bookkeeping fixes the total, not that it leaves as one photon (4471 h, 4472 g). This is energy conservation, which
  standard physics also has; it is not evidence for CPP.
- **The order is: the CP drops first, then the cloud sheds.** In his second bullet the cloud still holds the upper energy after the drop. So the drop can be
  quick and the shedding slow, which is what the measured lifetime needs (a drop at the moment of the trigger, then about 1.6 ns of light). (Claude's reading of
  the order.)

## 3. What sets the colour: the excess at an orbital's rhythm gives the wrong one (script, part 1)

Suppose, as Claude's hypothetical, the shed excess rang on at the rhythm of one of the orbitals: the upper one it came from, or the lower one whose share is
retained. For hydrogen's n = 2 → 1 (Lyman-α, 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz):
- each orbital's energy ÷ h: n = 2, 8.2 × 10¹⁴ Hz (a third of the colour); n = 1, 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz (four-thirds of it). (Energy ÷ h depends on where the energy
  zero is set. These values take it at ionisation, using binding energies. With rest energy included, each orbital's rhythm is about 1.24 × 10²⁰ Hz, some 5 × 10⁴

  orbital's absolute rhythm cannot be a radiating rhythm in its own right.)

  Bohr formulas, not two confirmations.)

The colour is neither orbital's rhythm. It is the *difference* of the two energy rhythms: 3.29 × 10¹⁵ − 8.2 × 10¹⁴ = 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz. So the excess cannot ring
at either orbital's rhythm. Two branches remain:
- **(i) the excess rings at its own energy ÷ h,** which is ΔE ÷ h. The colour is right, but only if the rhythm is fixed by the energy shed in total, not by what

- **(ii) the shedding involves both orbitals at once** (§4).

Both rest on 4470 b. His wording allows (ii): "the sustaining give-and-take of polarization-depolarization **between** the high-energy and low-energy orbitals
will be shed" (emphasis Claude's). It does not require it. In the sentence before, "between" marks a difference of energies, and read that way he says only

## 4. Two rhythms in one cloud keep one colour (script, part 2)

Suppose each orbital's give-and-take runs at a rhythm equal to its energy ÷ h. After the drop, the cloud holds two shares at once: the lower share, sustained at
the lower rhythm, and the unsustained excess, still at the upper rhythm while it lasts. Wherever the two overlap, their interplay rises and falls at the
difference of the rhythms, ΔE ÷ h (as two notes close in pitch give a loudness that rises and falls at the difference; in the atom the oscillating quantity is
the density, which is quadratic). In a toy cloud (not CPP dynamics):
- the interplay runs at exactly the difference at every moment of the emission (to the script's numerical precision, 2 × 10⁻⁶ in his order, 7 × 10⁻⁶ in the
  hand-over; this is an identity for fixed rhythms, not a test), however much excess is left: the mixture sets the strength, not the rate;
- adding the same constant to both rhythms (a different energy zero, rest energy included) changes nothing: only the difference matters. For the same reason, a
  single orbital's give-and-take must not itself radiate at its own rhythm. His "self-sustaining system of lossless resonance" is the CPP form of that (in
  standard physics a single state's charge cloud is static). Only the overlap of two orbitals' give-and-take may radiate. The radiating quantity must therefore
  be quadratic in the two, like a density (|ψ|²), not their sum. Two notes heard in air give no tone at the difference frequency; the beat is only in the
  loudness. What in the cloud does the squaring is owed (§6 j);
- in his order (the CP drops first, so the lower share is fully there from the start), the strength fades exponentially from the start, consistent with the
  measured rise no slower than the excitation, then fade (4464 c; 4462 §2, 4463 §3, with the caveats there). (This order is not available in standard physics,
  where a state's shares add to one. A fully excited atom there starts with no oscillating charge; see below.) A gradual hand-over would start from nothing and
  peak at 0.69 of the decay time instead.

If each orbital's rhythm stays fixed while its share changes, and the two do not pull each other, this meets both timing requirements: "the same duration"
(4473 §3, 4474 §3) and equal to h ÷ ΔE (4473 a), the same in every atom. The condition has moved from the arcs' stiffness to the two give-and-takes. Each must
keep its rhythm whatever its share, and the two must not shift each other's rhythm, to about 4 × 10⁻⁸ per e-fold for Lyman-α (the chirp bound, 4462 §2). That
is a linearity condition. The toy's fixed rate is built in, not derived. The arcs' discharge then supplies the energy; the pace is the beat between the two
rhythms. (Claude's reading.)

For the colour, this is the standard-physics mechanism (4460 §5: the charge cloud of an atom caught between two states oscillates at exactly ΔE ÷ h, however
much of each state it holds), read into his picture. It is a fact for CPP to match, not evidence for CPP. For the fading it is not the standard mechanism.
Semiclassical (neoclassical) theory, where the oscillating cloud itself radiates, predicts that a fully excited atom does not start radiating, that the
lifetime depends on how far the atom was excited, so the pulse rises and falls rather than fading from the start, and that the frequency shifts slightly as
the mixture changes (Crisp and Jaynes, Phys. Rev. 179, 1253, 1969). Experiment ruled those out: coherently excited rubidium decayed as simple exponentials with
times independent of the fraction excited, consistent with the quantized field and not with the neoclassical theory (Gibbs, Phys. Rev. Lett. 29, 459, 1972;
Phys. Rev. A 8, 456, 1973). Standard physics gets the exponential from the start from the vacuum field (Wigner–Weisskopf). His order (the lower share full from
the drop) would avoid the first two in a different way, but only with the bookkeeping question of §8 answered.

It is also convenient, and marked: it moves the whole timing problem of 4470–4474 onto one item already owed, **why each orbital's give-and-take runs at its
energy ÷ h, with the same h for every orbital** (4470 b). If the rhythm grew with energy in any other way (a different constant per orbital, or not in
proportion), the difference would not be ΔE ÷ h, and lines would not fall where they do. And it leaves open what sets how fast the excess is shed (the
lifetime, and its trend with frequency, 4470 h).

## 7. Reply to his message

That's a real step. Putting the energy in the cloud and having the CP drop first gives a clean account of where the photon's energy comes from: the cloud still
holds the upper orbital's energy, the lower orbital keeps its share, and what's left over, the difference, has nothing holding it and leaves. The pieces don't
need to be tuned to add up; the two orbitals fix the total. One check on that: the electron moves faster in the lower orbital, so its extra motion has to come
out of the same account, or what's shed isn't exactly the difference.

The colour is the harder part. If the leftover rang on at the upper orbital's own rhythm, for hydrogen's ultraviolet line that's only a third of the right
colour. The right colour is the difference between the two orbitals' rhythms. That leaves two ways: either the leftover rings at a rate set by its own energy,
which is right only if that rate doesn't sink as the leftover drains; or each orbital's give-and-take runs at a rate set by its energy, and the two run
together in the cloud while the leftover is shed, beating against each other at exactly the right colour. That beat doesn't drift as the leftover fades: only
its strength fades. That would move the timing problem of the last few patches onto one question, provided the two rhythms don't pull on each other, and
provided the cloud's starting state squares with your energy accounting. Standard physics hit trouble with this kind of picture for the fading, and I've
written that down.

It's also the convenient answer, because it's how standard physics gets the colour (not the fading), and it hands everything to one question we already owe:
why each orbital's rhythm is its energy divided by h, the same h for every orbital. I've marked that, and asked you three things about what goes on in the
cloud while the leftover is shed.
