## 1. His answer

- **Which-way:** a which-way detector interacts with and absorbs part of the photon or particle, so it collapses to the detector.
- **What a photon is (his postulate):**
  - the envelope of ZBW oscillations, each producing a plane of EM waves, DP arcs perpendicular to DP separation;
  - **its frequency is set by the envelope's duration, not by the ZBW frequency:** a short burst of fast oscillations across a high potential
    difference for high energy, a longer, slower packet for low energy.
- **How it is emitted:**
  - a collision throws the orbital electron's central CP out of resonance;
  - it falls through the nucleus and out the other side, reverses as its kinetic energy is exhausted, and oscillates back and forth, each pass leaving
    a wake of perpendicular DP arc-separation;
  - it loses energy to radiation (E and B perpendicular to each other and to the propagation, the direction set by the collision) until it settles
    into the new orbital.
- **The radiated pattern is toroidal around the electron's travel axis and localized front to back**, analogous to the inertial propagation of mass,
  but radiating rather than conserving.
- **A mass is the entirety of its stable orbital and conservative, non-radiating ZBW oscillations**, kept by a resonance of emission and reabsorption
  akin to the inertia process.

## 2. What fits

- **A photon as a packet.** Light from an atom comes in packets of finite length, lasting about as long as the decay: 1.6 ns for hydrogen's
  ultraviolet Lyman-α, a packet about 0.48 m long (script part 1).
- **A ring of transverse fields from a swinging charge.** A charge swinging along a line radiates as a dipole: a ring around the swing's axis,
  strongest at right angles to it and zero along it, with the electric field along the axis and at right angles to the direction of travel (for a
  linear swing; transitions that change the atom's spin direction give circularly polarized light, emitted along the axis too). His toroidal wake
  around the electron's travel axis, with E and B perpendicular to the propagation, is that ring. **This answers 4444's point about transverse fields
  in part:** the transverse E and B come from the CP's swing, and the polarization follows its axis.
- **Mass and light as the same kind of oscillation,** radiating versus non-radiating. This fits 4443's relay picture: a mass's stable, conservative
  oscillation is a candidate for the rest beat at mc²/h that 4443 asked for.

## 3. What does not fit as stated, and what could make it fit (script parts 1, 2 and 4)

**The frequency is the carrier inside the packet.** The measured frequency of emitted light is ΔE/h, and it is sharp. The envelope's duration sets
the packet's length and the spread of frequencies (the linewidth), not the frequency itself. Reading his rule as frequency ≈ 1/τ (Claude's reading;
he gave no formula):
- **Hydrogen Lyman-α:** carrier 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz; envelope 1.6 ns. The packet holds about 4 million carrier cycles, and the linewidth is 10⁸ Hz, a
  fraction 4 × 10⁻⁸ of the frequency. 1/τ would be 6 × 10⁸ Hz, 4 million times too low.
- **Iron-57's Mössbauer gamma ray:** 14.4 keV, carrier 3.5 × 10¹⁸ Hz, envelope 141 ns, a packet 42 m long holding about 5 × 10¹¹ cycles.

**There is a real trend behind "short burst for high energy".** For transitions of the same kind, the decay rate grows as the cube of the frequency
times the square of the transition's strength, so higher-frequency lines do tend to decay faster: hydrogen's 2p lasts 1.6 ns, its 3p 5.4 ns. Iron-57's
line is a nuclear magnetic transition of a different kind, a counterexample across kinds, not within one. The trend does not set the frequency: the
frequency is still ΔE/h, and the envelope sets the linewidth.

**What could make it fit (Claude's proposal).** A charge radiates at the frequency it swings. If the CP swings through the atom at ΔE/h during the
decay (about 2.5 × 10¹⁵ times a second for Lyman-α, about 4 million swings over the decay), the light comes out at the right frequency, and the envelope
is the decay time. A strong check (script part 4): a charge swinging at the Lyman-α frequency loses its energy to radiation in about 0.66 ns. Weighted
by hydrogen's measured transition strength (0.416, with the states' multiplicity ratio 1/3), that gives a lifetime of 1.60 ns, the measured 2p
lifetime. So a swing at ΔE/h with hydrogen's measured strength gives both the frequency and the packet length. In standard quantum mechanics, the
charge of an atom caught between two states oscillates at exactly ΔE/h (a fully excited atom has no such oscillation until something starts the
decay).

**Two weaknesses to flag in the swing-through-the-nucleus picture:**
- **Harmonics.** A charge falling through a point charge moves non-sinusoidally, so it would also radiate at 2f, 3f and so on. A single line shows
  none.
- **The shape of the states.** Straight-line motion through the centre resembles an s state, while Lyman-α goes from 2p to 1s.

**A ring around the swing does not go forward along it.** He says two things: the toroid surrounds the electron's travel axis, and the packet is
localized front to back with the forward direction set by the collision. If the collision sets the swing axis, propagation along the collision is
exactly the direction a dipole does not radiate. And a ring going out all around the axis is not localized to one forward direction. What is measured:
a single photon is detected in one direction, with probability spread over the ring (as sin²θ for a linear swing), and the atom recoils the opposite
way. So "forward along the collision" fits only if forward is at right angles to the swing. How a single photon is localized, against the ring as the
pattern of probabilities, is open.

**Which-way marking need not absorb anything.** Fringes vanish whenever the path can be told, even when nothing is absorbed and every particle arrives
whole at the screen:
- **A lossless polarization mark:** a half-wave plate at one slit turns that path's polarization by 90°. Nothing is absorbed, and every photon
  arrives whole, but the fringes vanish, because light of crossed polarizations does not interfere (the Fresnel–Arago laws). In the quantum-eraser
  experiment of Walborn and others (2002), quarter-wave plates at the slits marked the path in the polarization of one photon of an entangled pair. The
  fringes came back when the partner photon was passed through a polarizer and the photons were counted in coincidence.
- **Internal-state marks:** atoms in an interferometer carry their path in an internal state, with kicks far too small to explain the loss, and the
  fringes vanish (Dürr, Nonn and Rempe, 1998).
- **The quantitative rule** (Englert 1996): fringe visibility V and path distinguishability D obey V² + D² ≤ 1 (script part 3). With D = 1 (full path
  information) there are no fringes; with D = 0.5 the visibility is at most 0.87.

Absorbing part of the particle at one slit unbalances the two paths and lowers the visibility only modestly, and each particle is still detected
whole, in one click at one point. Absorbing all of one path removes the fringes only by removing that path, which is not what the experiments do. So
"absorbs part" does not explain the measurements. What destroys the fringes is that the path is recorded somewhere, by any means. In CPP terms, the
question is how a mark left on one path, removing nothing, stops the two parts from adding.

**The trigger.** Atoms decay in empty space without collisions: the lifetimes are measured in isolated atomic beams. So the trigger must be present in
vacuum. In CPP the DP Sea itself is a natural candidate (Claude's proposal), in the role vacuum fluctuations play in standard physics.

## 6. Answer to his "What do you think?"

Much of this fits what's measured. Light from an atom does come in packets: hydrogen's ultraviolet packets last about a billionth and a half of a
second and are about half a metre long. A charge swinging back and forth radiates in a ring around the swing, with electric and magnetic fields at
right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, and the polarization lined up with the swing. So his picture gives light's sideways
vibration, one of my open points. And mass and light as the same kind of oscillation, one radiating and one not, fits the relay picture.

Some points don't fit as he put them. The light's frequency isn't set by how long the packet lasts. It's the steady oscillation inside the packet;
hydrogen's packet holds about 4 million cycles. Higher-energy lines of the same kind do tend to come in shorter packets, so his sense of a trend is
right, but the length sets only how sharp the colour is. His picture can fit if the electron's swings are the oscillation. A charge swinging at the
light's own frequency, with hydrogen's measured strength, gives off its energy in 1.6 billionths of a second, exactly the measured packet. Also, a
swinging charge sends its light out in a ring around the swing, not forward along it, so the direction he gives the photon needs another look.

And a which-way marker needn't absorb anything to wipe out the fringes. A clear plate that turns one slit's light sideways absorbs nothing, every
photon arrives whole, and the fringes vanish. What destroys them is the path being marked, by any means. Also, atoms decay in empty space with no
collisions, so the trigger has to be something always present; the DP Sea could be that.
