## 3. Where it conflicts: "acceleration per unit mass rising with realness" (script parts 1–2)

**In GR the speed effect is direction-dependent (script part 1).** The coordinate acceleration of a particle moving at v in a weak field (in
isotropic coordinates) is:

| v | moving across the field | moving along the field | averaged over directions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | g × 1.010 | g × 0.970 | g × 0.997 |
| 0.5 | g × 1.250 | g × 0.250 | g × 0.917 |
| 0.9 | g × 1.810 | g × −1.430 | g × 0.730 |
| 1.0 (light) | g × 2.000 | g × −2.000 | g × 0.667 |

Sideways motion is pulled more as speed rises (light: twice), which is the extra bending. Motion along the field gains less, and in these
coordinates even reverses above about 0.58c. That reversal is a coordinate effect: light's coordinate speed falls toward the mass. The effect
is not a uniform "falls faster". For bound matter, the internal motions, together with the binding that holds them, fall exactly like the
whole: the equivalence principle holds in GR.

**A uniform rule would break equal fall (script part 2).** The nucleons inside nuclei move with v² of a few percent of c², and the amount
differs from element to element. If acceleration per unit mass rose as 1 + k·v² regardless of direction, titanium and platinum would fall
differently by about k × 4 × 10⁻³ (illustrative internal speeds). MICROSCOPE finds them equal to about 10⁻¹⁵. That excludes the rule by a
factor of about 4 × 10¹² for k = 1, and still by 4 × 10⁶ for k = 10⁻⁶. **His "negligible at non-relativistic speeds".** Matter's insides are not slow: nucleons in nuclei move at about 0.2–0.3c (v² ≈ 0.04), and
platinum's innermost electrons at about Zα ≈ 0.57c. If the effect grows as vⁿ, to be "significant near c", keeping the titanium–platinum difference
below 10⁻¹⁵ at v ≈ 0.2c (with about a 10% difference between them) needs n of about 20 or more (script part 2): effectively a switch that turns on
only near light speed. **So the realized organization must fall exactly as its mass-energy
falls:** adding partly real mass adds weight and inertia in the same proportion, as ordinary mass does. The speed effect on a passing particle
must take GR's direction-dependent form, not a rise in susceptibility.

**For light (script part 3).** The refraction by WC-DP-SEA-PV's index (n = 1 + 2U) already gives light's full measured bending, 4GM/(bc²).
Cassini bounds any departure at about 2 × 10⁻⁵. A further realness bending of light on top of that refraction would overshoot, so it must
already be part of the same accounting, not an addition.

## 6. Answer to his "What do you think?"

He is right that a fast particle bends more as its speed rises; that difference is both calculated and measured, an extra bending that is the
same angle at every speed. His first component, the slower inner limb where the PSR is smaller, is just what's needed. Even his "falls faster"
is right for a particle moving sideways past a mass, which relativity pulls harder as its speed rises, up to twice as hard for light. But that is
all the slower-space effect, his first component; the second can't add more on top.

The new concept, that acceleration per unit mass rises as DPs become more real, can't be right as a general rule. Matter's insides are not slow:
nucleons move at about a fifth of light speed and platinum's innermost electrons at more than half, by different amounts in different elements. For
any rule that matters near light speed, titanium and platinum would fall at different rates, far beyond what experiments allow, unless the rule
switches on only very close to light speed. In relativity the effect of speed
depends on direction. A particle moving sideways past a mass is pulled more; one moving straight toward it gains less. For matter held together, the motions together with the binding that holds them fall exactly like the whole, so every material falls alike. And for light, the slower light speed near a mass already gives the
whole measured bending, so there is no room for an extra push from realness on top of it.
