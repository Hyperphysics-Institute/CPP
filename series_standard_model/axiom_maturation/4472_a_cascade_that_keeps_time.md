## 3. Is a decay in stages plausible? In outline, yes, with a timing requirement (script part 1)

**Plausible in outline.** A collision triggers a change in one place, which triggers its neighbours, all through the same energy difference: that is a cascade,
and it is the sequence 4471 §3–§4 said a fine line needs, instead of everything at once. Cascades of this kind are common in nature (a row of dominoes, a flame
front, a nerve impulse); that is plausibility, not CPP support. It answers 4471 §5's "all at once or one after another" tentatively in favour of one after
another ("I suppose they could"). Note that his cascade's units are now "sections of the orbital" set off by the collided electron CP, not the central CP and
each polarized DP that 4471 §5 asked about.

**A single sweep is far too short.** Light speed is the fastest any relay can go (4415, 4325); a collapse carried by CP or DP motion would go at the CP's V_i or
slower (4325, 4326). At light speed one crossing of an orbital 0.1–0.3 nm across takes about a thousandth of a Lyman-α period; at the electron's orbital speed
(about 2.2 × 10⁶ m/s, c/137) it takes about 0.1–0.3 of a period (script). Either way it is far short of the lifetime, and the conclusion holds for any speed above
about 0.2 m/s. A sharp line needs the emission to last many cycles: of order millions if each photon lasts about the lifetime (4466 §4); at least of order ten
thousand from the computed Doppler bound for a hot hydrogen discharge (4467 §2); in step and without gaps (4471 §3). A cascade that sweeps once and is done would
give a short pulse, smeared in colour, however many stages it had.

**So something other than a single sweep must keep time.** The options (the list is Claude's):
- **Crawl:** a single sweep lasting the 1.6 ns lifetime would move at about 0.06–0.2 m/s, which would need a cause for so slow a front.
- **Wait at each stage:** discrete stages, each waiting a local relaxation time before setting off the next; slow on average without a slow front.
- **Cycle:** go round and round, giving up a share each round, each round one period of the colour. A round of 0.6–1.9 nm in 4.06 × 10⁻¹⁶ s means a front of
  about 1.5–4.6 × 10⁶ m/s (c/200 to c/65), not light speed; at c it would go round about 60–200 times per period. So cycling also needs a front far slower than
  light, though not as slow as a crawl. That speed is of the order of the electron's orbital speed, a coincidence of scale, not evidence.
- **Quick trigger, then each section rings on:** a fast sweep sets off every section within a small fraction of a cycle, leaving them nearly in step; each section
  then keeps swinging on its own, and the emission lasts as long as one section's ringing, not as long as the front takes. That is the 4470 §4 eigenmode with
  spatial extent; in 4471's terms the sections sit side by side, in step, and the photon is as long as one section's ringing.

The cycle branch is the convenient one for Claude (it joins his cascade to the eigenmode clock of 4470 §4); the last option fits 4470 §4 as well. Which, if any,
fits his picture is asked (§6).

- **The collision trigger.** Isolated atoms in high-vacuum beams decay at the measured 1.6 ns lifetime with no collisions with other atoms; in a gas, collisions

## 4. The Brusselator: one part fits (script part 3)

The Brusselator (Prigogine and Lefever's model reaction, 1968) is a chemical scheme that oscillates by itself above a threshold (B = 1 + A²), feeding on its own
products. Below threshold, disturbed from rest, it rings and dies away (for (A − 1)² < B < (A + 1)²; further away it just relaxes). Paul LaViolette's
subquantum kinetics is built on a reaction–diffusion system that extends this family (Claude's general knowledge; to be verified, §7).
- **What fits:** below threshold it keeps nearly the same rate while dying away, for the disturbance sizes tried (script: periods 6.49, 6.49 and 6.48 for
  disturbances of 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0). That is the property a staged photon emission needs: a rate held while the release fades (4461 §4, 4451's "mass on a
  spring").
- **What is generic:** that property belongs to any weakly damped oscillator. The Brusselator's own features, feeding on itself and oscillating by itself above
  threshold, are not used here. A closer Brusselator-style picture would be the above-threshold oscillation running while its feed lasts, the stored energy
  difference being the feed, and stopping when the feed falls below threshold; that is another analogy option.
- **The fine-tuning:** near threshold it rings many cycles before fading (linear prediction about 16 at B = 1.98; simulated, about 18). The number of cycles is
  about 1 ÷ (π × (1 + A² − B)), so the 3.9 million cycles of a Lyman-α photon would need B within about 8 × 10⁻⁸ of threshold (script).
- **What sets the rate:** in the Brusselator, its own make-up. In a CPP analogue, Claude reads it as set by the energy difference; his "same energy higher to lower
  differential" explains why the collapse propagates, not what sets the rate, so the rate–ΔE link is Claude's and owed (4470 b).

It is an analogy, not evidence: a chemical reaction's concentrations are not DP arcs, and the Brusselator does not keep track of energy as a photon must.

## 8. Answer to his "What do you think?"

Yes, on the qualifier, and he's right that it was already in the record, implicitly; it's good to have it stated.

A decay in stages is plausible in outline, and it's the "one after another" a sharp line needs; whether it stays in step and without gaps isn't yet said. A
collision sets off one part of the orbital, which sets off its neighbours, all through the same energy drop: a cascade, like dominoes or a flame front. But one
sweep through the atom is far too quick. Even at the electron's orbital speed it crosses the atom in a fraction of one cycle, and a sharp line needs millions of
cycles. So something other than a single sweep has to keep time. It could crawl very slowly, wait at each stage, go round and round giving up a share each round,
or set every part swinging at once and let each part ring on for a long time. I've listed those; which, if any, fits his picture is his to say.

His friend's Brusselator fits one part of this. It's a chemical reaction that oscillates on its own, and below its threshold, when it's disturbed, it rings and
dies away while keeping nearly the same rate. That's the kind of steady rhythm a photon's emission needs. But that property belongs to any gently damped
oscillator, and to ring millions of times before fading it would have to sit within a few parts in a hundred million of its threshold. It's an analogy, not
evidence.

On the increments, he already has them adding up to the photon's energy, so each is a small share. What's open is what "E = hf" means for each share, and his ħ
postulate is his own earlier ruling (4320), later restated as the action of a whole swing (4325). I've asked: as the collapse passes from one
part of the orbital to the next, does each part give up its share at once or keep swinging as it empties, does the collapse pass through once or keep going
round, and what sets the rhythm at which the energy leaves?
