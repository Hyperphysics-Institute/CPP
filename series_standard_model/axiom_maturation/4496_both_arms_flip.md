## 3. What remains

### 3.1 The same-flip condition

Route (A) keeps the fringes only if each arm's flips **do not depend on whether the core went down it.** If the core's own charges add anything to the flips in
its arm — a stronger turn, a different angle, more DPs turned — beyond what the arcs do, that difference is a which-path record. A small difference per DP
adds up: a million DPs each differing by a thousandth of a radian leave 88% of the fringes (4494 row T2), and over a real flight the residue must distinguish
the arms by less than about 10⁻²⁰ per Moment (4492 row S1; Moment = t_P, 1 ms flight).

**His own words already give most of it** (Claude's reading, D-15). If "the arc-cloud is the totality of the KE/Momentum/action," the core carries no momentum of
its own, so the momentum part of the flip is the arcs' in both arms. What is left is only the core's electric pull, the 4494 mechanism ("the opposite charge
is drawn toward the atomic core"), which for a neutral atom is confined to roughly a tube of atomic radius along its path (4494 §3.1). That tube is the
specific asymmetry §5 asks about.

### 3.2 The empty-arm rule

If the arcs in the empty arm flip DPs as the core's arm does, something real happens in the empty arm on every pass. The corpus has met this before (D-10):
4492 §2 noted that "an instant change to the arcs in the far path moves momentum density across the gap at once ... a feature to name in 4464 (d)"; 4477 §3
recorded the photon's empty-wave cost, that "the far half would carry none"; 4494 §3.3 (T4) named probes in the empty arm, such as an ion interferometer.
His "the arc-cloud is the totality of the KE/Momentum/action" puts momentum in the arcs, the opposite of the pilot wave (4464 §2), so the cost is real.

**What quantum mechanics says** (Claude's statement). A single-shot probe in either arm either registers nothing or registers the whole atom, and the atom is
then found in that arm. No probe registers a share of the atom's momentum in an arm where the atom is not then found. Weak measurements read nonzero values in
both arms, but only as ensemble averages (Aharonov, Albert and Vaidman 1988; Kocsis et al., Science 332, 1170, 2011; Danan et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 111,
240402, 2013; the neutron "Cheshire cat," Denkmayr et al., Nat. Commun. 5, 4492, 2014). Protective measurement (Aharonov, Anandan and Vaidman, Phys. Rev. A
47, 4616, 1993) reads the wave on a single system only under protection. Neither gives a single-shot reading in the arm the atom was not found in. The closest
standard precedent is the guided-particle literature: Hardy (Phys. Lett. A 167, 11, 1992) on empty waves, and Englert, Scully, Süssmann and Walther (Z.
Naturforsch. 47a, 1175, 1992) on "surrealistic" trajectories, tested by Mahler et al. (Sci. Adv. 2, e1501466, 2016). Their point: in a theory where a definite
particle is guided by a wave, a which-path detector can fire in the arm the particle did not take; the record follows the wave, not the particle.

**The fork, in his terms** (Claude's statement). He says gas "will" take momentum but not in which arm. Either (i) the arcs in the empty arm never transfer
momentum to gas there, so the empty arm is inert to gas, as the pilot wave's empty branch is to particle positions; or (ii) they can bump a gas atom, and then
a bump in arm B must coincide with the atom being found in B, which needs 4478's per-quantum gathering ("gathered at once") to bring the core to B. Either
way, collision rates must match the single-path cross-section Hornberger et al. used (4479 §3).

## 4. "Conservation of action," at its strongest

Action is not conserved in standard physics; it is the quantity a path makes stationary, not a conserved charge. The strongest reading of his conjecture
(Claude's) is the standard point that **conservation laws produce entanglement**: a pair with definite total momentum has parts whose momenta are tied, as in
the 1935 Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen state. That is his own earlier reason, "to conserve momentum and energy" (4492 §2), with 4481 §2's condition "when the kick
could in principle be read" (D-10).

A second reading, limited to the quantum's own members: in the semiclassical (path-integral) picture the phase along each arm is S/ħ, and the fringes come from
the difference between the arms' actions; a term that keeps each member's action accounted for keeps the arms' phases comparable at the screen. This must not
be read as "entanglement keeps the fringes sharp": in quantum mechanics, entanglement of the atom with anything outside it is what removes the fringes
(Englert), and route (A) works precisely because the trail is not entangled with the path. The link to his ħ unit (4480 b): ħ is the unit of action. Filed as
his conjecture ("perhaps"); not adopted.

## 7. Reply to the founder

That's the route on which CPP can stay in line with standard physics, given one condition. The arcs in the empty arm flip their DPs too, so the trail need
not tell which way the atom went. It also answers the far-path puzzle: the arcs belong to the atom because they carry its momentum, not because the core
touched them.

The condition: each arm's flips must not depend on whether the core went down it. If the arcs carry all the momentum, as you say, what's left is the core's
own electric pull on the DPs right beside it, in a thin tube about the width of an atom. Those DPs must not end up flipped differently from the ones the arcs
alone pass in the other arm.

There's also a cost to keep in view. If the arcs really do something in the empty arm, then in quantum mechanics no detector there may ever catch a share of
the atom's momentum on its own; it either finds the whole atom or nothing.

On "conservation of action": action itself isn't conserved, but your earlier point stands, that conservation laws are what tie particles together. It
shouldn't be read as entanglement keeping the stripes sharp, because entanglement with anything outside the atom is what washes them out.
