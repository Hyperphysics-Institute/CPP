## 3. Cohesion defines rest mass: relativity's invariant mass (script parts 1 and 3)

His criterion matches relativity's **invariant mass**: a system's rest mass is its total energy in the frame where it is at rest overall,
√(E² − p²). Internal motions count; the motion of the whole does not.
- **Two balls flying apart.** Two balls of rest mass 1 flying apart at 0.6c make a system of rest mass 2.5, not 2: their motion is internal to the
  system and counts.
- **An electron passing the Earth.** Taken together with the Earth, the electron's kinetic energy (0.25 of its rest mass at 0.6c, 1.29 at 0.9c)
  adds that much to the system's rest mass, to first order (the correction, −p²/2M_Earth, is negligible), as his Earth–electron remark says.
- **A hot ball and a charged capacitor.** Their thermal and field energy are internal, so they count as rest mass and feel the push (his 4408,
  4417).

**His cohesion is GR's internal stress.** In GR, cohesion is not idle. Free particles moving in every direction at speed v, each falling by
GR's rule (more across, less along), fall on average at (1 − v²/3)g, not g (script part 3). A bound body falls at g, with all its internal energy
weighing, only when the stresses that hold it together are counted (von Laue, Tolman). His "a force on one part is communicated to the other
parts" plays exactly that role. So his cohesion is not only a picture of why the parts add up: it is the piece GR needs for a body's internal
energy to fall as one. This is Claude's identification, to be checked in CPP terms.

## 4. One subtlety: what "acts on the rest mass" has to mean (script part 2)

4436 found that the required push is an acceleration of g(m/E)² = g/γ². Across the motion, this is the rest-mass term of the Jacobi index, which
fixes the path's shape. Along the motion, the same value comes from GR's coordinate acceleration (4436). It is not a Newtonian force mg applied to
the moving body. With the body's inertia (γm across the motion, γ³m along), a force mg would give accelerations g/γ across and g/γ³ along. With
the full turning, that gives 1.366 g across and 0.150 g along at v = 0.5, where general relativity needs 1.250 and 0.250.

As a force, the required push is (m²/E)g across and E·g along. No single force proportional to g on one mass, m or E, gives it. So "the push acts on
the rest mass" must mean, in CPP terms, that the push changes the drift by g times the square of the rest fraction, (m/E)², in every direction. His
axial-only connection (the rest mass sees only the SSV_net along its travel) may be the start of a mechanism. How it gives (m/E)² is owed.

## 7. Answer to his "What do you think?"

I think he has it, and it is the cleanest statement yet. His rule is that whatever moves inside a body's own frame, held together with it, is part of
its rest mass and feels gravity's push. The motion of the body as a whole doesn't feel the push. That is exactly relativity's idea of rest mass, the
total energy of a system seen from the frame where it is at rest. His Earth–electron example is the textbook case: take the two together, and the
electron's energy of motion becomes part of the pair's rest mass.

His reason, that the parts hold together and pass force among themselves, does real work. In relativity, a cloud of particles flying freely in all
directions would fall slightly slower than everything else. It falls at the normal rate only when the forces holding it together are counted. His
cohesion is that piece.

One thing needs care. "The push acts on the rest mass" can't mean an ordinary force equal to the rest mass times gravity's pull, pushing on the moving
electron. The electron resists being pushed differently along and across its motion, and that would bend it slightly wrong. It has to mean that the
push changes the electron's motion by gravity's pull times the square of the fraction of its energy that is rest mass, the same in every direction.
His remark that the rest mass sees only the net signal along its travel may be where that comes from; that's still to be worked out.
