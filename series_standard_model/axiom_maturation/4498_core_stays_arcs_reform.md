## 2. The block: consistent, under two conditions

His arcs funnel through the open slit and reform; their energy is not lost but carried through with the atom. That gives his 4497 answer ("all the energy
through only one slit") a mechanism — a local analogue of 4478's gathering, unfolding in time where 4478's is at once. TODO-4497-c answered in his terms.
Two conditions follow (Claude's statements):

- **Momentum at the mask, exactly as conservation requires.** In quantum mechanics the mask (open-slit edges and block, one rigid body) takes, from each atom
  that passes, exactly the opposite of that atom's change of momentum, distributed as the single-slit diffraction (Bohr's recoiling slit; measured by
  Schmidt et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 111, 103201, 2013, and Liu et al., Nature Photonics 9, 120, 2015). The arcs that meet the block carry a share of the atom's
  forward momentum (about half for an even split, 4497 §2). As they reform through the open slit they must hand that share back to the atom and leave none
  in the block. Otherwise each passing atom loses about half its forward momentum to the block, contradicting the measured open-slit wavelength (4497 §4).
- **The core into the block.** When the core is the part that meets the block, the atom is absorbed. Its arcs through the open slit must then be gathered
  back too, so that nothing with the atom's energy arrives at the screen without an atom. If the open slit is far from the block, a reforming that travels no
  faster than light cannot do this in time; spacelike single-quantum tests (Guerreiro et al. 2012, 4477 §3) point to "at once."

The far-field pattern of the open slit must equal that of a single slit with nothing beside it, which his "fanned out beam we expect to see" is consistent
with.

## 3. The core stays: a departure if the empty arm's kick can ever be readable

His answer chooses the nudge, not the gathering (4497 §3).

**Read most favourably (D-15).** "Cannot be bumped hard enough" can mean that the arcs in the arm without the core never give a gas atom a readable kick —
only one lost in the gas atom's own spread of momentum (4481 §2). Readable kicks would then happen only in the arm the core is in. On that reading the gas
record and the core's arm agree, and quantum mechanics' correlation rule and the single-path collision rate both hold. Fork (i) of 4496 §3.2 (no momentum to
gas at all) is excluded by his "it will cause an absolute loss or gain of momentum"; only this weakened version, which allows kicks but never readable ones,
survives. Against this reading: "The big collision with the other path/slit DP-arcs will be to change the trajectory of the atom core traveling in the other
path" implies a kick large enough to change the core's course. And his "So, its trajectory would change, but it would not become part of the beam that went
through the other side" is ambiguous: "its" may be the gas atom or the empty branch. §5 asks which he means.

**If the empty arm's kick can be readable** (Claude's statement). Take gas in arm B only, and suppose the arcs there give a gas atom a kick that could be
noticed. In quantum mechanics, an atom detected right after such a kick leaves by arm B, every time (4497 §3). In his picture the core may be in arm A when the
arcs kick the gas in B, so a readable recoil in B would sometimes be followed by the atom leaving by arm A. That is a which-path record and a detection that
disagree, which quantum mechanics forbids.

**The reading in which the recoil carries no path information** (Claude's statement). If the arcs are in arm B on every pass, a recoil in B happens whether
the core went down A or B; inside CPP the gas record then carries no which-path information. But the observable that quantum mechanics constrains — a
readable recoil located in B — would still be followed by atoms from A, so it still conflicts with quantum mechanics' prediction. It differs from
complementarity in one further respect: fringes would come back when atoms are sorted by the gas recoil. With gas in one arm only, quantum mechanics says
atoms sorted by a readable recoil in B show the single-slit pattern of arm B, shifted by the kick, with no fringes; a shared kick to the whole cohort would
instead give shifted fringes that sorting recovers (the Chapman et al. 1995 kind of recovery). Without sorting, both pictures can lose the fringes once the
kick spread is at least about h/d.

**Rates** (Claude's statement; the level of agreement not checked in this patch). Hornberger et al. (Phys. Rev. Lett. 90, 160401, 2003) found the measured
decoherence pressures in agreement with the standard collision rate n·σ·v, using cross-sections calculated from the van der Waals interaction, counted once
per molecule. In their Talbot–Lau setup the gas fills every path and each molecule is coherent over several grating slits, so counting collisions per path
would multiply the rate by roughly the number of slits. So the empty arm's chance of a bump must be proportional to its share: the probability of a scatter in
B equals the probability weight of arm B, which equals the momentum share when both arms move at the same speed — in quantum mechanics, the Born rule.

**The test, stated.** With gas, or any position-recording scatterer, in one arm only, record in coincidence where the scattering happened and which arm the
atom leaves by (detectors close behind the arms, or the output ports of an interferometer whose paths are still apart) — or, in the far field, whether sorting
by recoil brings fringes back. Experiments with massive particles where scattering or a marker affects one or both arms, all consistent with quantum
mechanics: Pfau et al. (Phys. Rev. Lett. 73, 1223, 1994); Chapman et al. (Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 3783, 1995: scattering in both arms, sorted by photon
direction, not by position); Buks et al. (Nature 391, 871, 1998: a detector beside one arm; dephasing seen, no coincidence); Dürr, Nonn and Rempe (Nature 395,
33, 1998: marking with no kick); a neutron absorber in one arm (Summhammer, Rauch and Tuppinger, Phys. Rev. A 36, 4447, 1987). None known to the critic or
to Claude records, in coincidence, where a scatterer in one arm was hit and which arm the atom left by; the search stays owed and starts from these.

For single photons, the anticorrelation of a split photon at two detectors (4478 §2) is a detection result, not a test of where the photon scattered, and
4478 records that a model with the energy in one arm reproduces it. On Claude's reading a photon in CPP has no core: 4470 §1 describes it as "a volume of DP
arc-separation around the axis of travel" (the "central CP" there belongs to the emitting cohort). So the photon case does not test this one.

**Convenient branch (PD-008).** The gathering reading of 4497 §3 was convenient to Claude; his answer declines it. The gentler reading above is convenient in
the same way, since it keeps CPP in line with quantum mechanics; it is offered because his words allow it, and §5 asks.

## 7. Reply to the founder

On the block, that works: the arcs that meet the block funnel through the open slit and reform, so the passing atom keeps all its energy. Two conditions
follow. The slit plate is nudged by every atom that goes through and bends, which is ordinary physics; the arcs that hit the block must take back their share
of the atom's forward push as they reform, and leave none of it in the plate. And when it's the core that hits the block, the arcs that went through the open
slit have to be gathered back, so nothing with the atom's energy reaches the screen without an atom.

On the empty-arm bump, your answer is that the core stays in its arm. Whether that parts from quantum mechanics depends on one thing. If the arcs in the arm
without the core can ever kick a gas atom hard enough to be noticed, then yes: in quantum mechanics a noticed kick in one arm means the atom leaves by that
arm, every time, and in your picture it could leave by the other. If instead the empty arm only ever brushes the gas too lightly to leave a usable trace, your
picture and quantum mechanics agree. Either way it's testable: gas in one arm, a detector behind each arm, and a check of which detector the atom reaches
whenever a kick is noticed. I know of no experiment that has done exactly that yet.

(The reviewer corrected four things: the slit plate does get nudged by atoms that pass, so the condition is about the extra share only; your words also allow
the gentler reading; the question now includes detectors behind each arm; and I've listed the related experiments that have been done.) My question for you is
in §5.
