## 3. Three points to correct against the measurements (script parts 1–2, 4)

- **The edges' gravity is far too weak** (script part 1). Take a slit edge as 1 g of material, with light passing 1 μm away. Its gravitational
  deflection is about 3 × 10⁻²⁴ radian. The fringe angle for 500 nm light and slits 100 μm apart is about 5 × 10⁻³ radian, some 10²¹ times larger.
  Fringe positions are set by the openings' geometry and the wavelength, whatever the material. The material changes only finer details of
  intensity, and it does so through electromagnetic forces, never through gravity: van der Waals forces from a grating's bars for atoms and
  molecules (Grisenti et al. 1999), and edge conductivity, thickness and near-field effects for light. Light is diffracted at a slit by the
  electromagnetic response of the screen's charges; the textbook boundary picture is an idealization of that. Electrons are even diffracted by a
  standing wave of light, with no material edge at all (Kapitza–Dirac effect, observed by Freimund, Aflatooni and Batelaan, 2001), and atoms by gratings
  of light (Moskowitz et al. 1983; Gould et al. 1986). So his intuition that the edges interact with what passes has a real kernel; it is the
  gravity mechanism that fails.
- **A single slit makes its own pattern** (script part 2). If "no pattern" means no fine two-slit fringes, that is right. But a single slit gives a bright central band with dark minima where sin θ = mλ/a, for slit width a: at 1.4°,
  2.9° and 4.3° for 500 nm light through a 20 μm slit. Two slits add finer fringes, spaced λL/d, inside that single-slit band: 5 mm fringes within a
  50 mm envelope, for slits 100 μm apart and a screen at 1 m.
- **Light's measured fields are transverse.** Polarizers show it. Two crossed polarizers block light, which requires the oscillation to be across the
  direction of travel. In CPP, the founder's 2026-07-28 ruling has the DP arc displacement longitudinal, with the B field's circulation transverse
  (the right-hand rule). So "longitudinal" there is about the DPs' displacement, and CPP must still give the transverse fields and polarization that
  are measured. Longitudinal waves interfere too (sound does). So longitudinal is no obstacle to interference, but it is to polarization.

**On the beat and the Compton frequency** (script part 4). The light emitted when an electron drops between orbitals is far below the Compton frequency.
Hydrogen's strongest line, Lyman-α, is 2.5 × 10¹⁵ Hz, against the electron's Compton frequency of 1.2 × 10²⁰ Hz, a ratio of about 2 × 10⁻⁵ (exactly 3α²/8, since Lyman-α is ¾ of the Rydberg energy α²mc²/2). Emitted light is at ΔE/h whatever the internal mechanism, so the orbital oscillation that emits light is not the Compton-rate beat. The beat 4443 asks for belongs to the electron at rest, before any
orbit.

## 4. What must span both slits (script part 3)

His picture is that the particle occupies a volume and part of it passes each slit. The measured separations set how big that volume must be:
- in electron biprism experiments the two paths pass on either side of a filament well under a micrometre thick, a few micrometres apart, and single
  electrons build up the fringes one dot at a time, as his "many points" says (Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi 1976; Tonomura et al. 1989);
- in the 1975 neutron interferometer they were about 3 cm apart;
- in the atom interferometer of Kovachy and others (2015) they were **54 cm apart**, and the atoms still interfered. (The atoms came from a
  Bose–Einstein condensate, but the interference is of each atom with itself.)

So something belonging to the atom must be present on both paths, up to 54 cm apart, and stay coherent. That need not be one continuous volume: in
that experiment each path's packet is small, and the two packets are far apart. The options include:
- the particle's pattern splitting into two parts that later recombine;
- something that reaches both paths while the core takes one: the relay itself, the sea's arc-separation that carries the motion (4441), or the
  DI-bit field (in standard physics, pilot-wave theory takes this form: the particle takes one path and a guiding wave takes both);
- the core cloud itself, if it is far larger than an atom.

This is the key physical question.

**Which-way.** In the measurements, if anything records which slit the particle took, the fringes vanish. A picture with part through each slit must
also explain why recording the path destroys the pattern.
