### 3.2 What the experiments fix: one shared kick, felt in both arms

The standard account of this experiment is also a kick (Chapman, Hammond, Lenef, Schmiedmayer, Rubenstein, Smith and Pritchard, Phys. Rev. Lett. 75, 3783,
1995). A scattered photon carries away momentum, and the atom takes up the difference; because momentum is conserved, the atom's final momentum records the
photon's direction. The kick multiplies the atom's guiding wave in **both** arms by the same factor exp(i q·x), which takes a different phase at each arm, so the two arms, a
distance d apart where the photon hits, pick up a phase difference that depends on d. (Chapman et al. used a three-grating interferometer, with the path
separation set by where along it the light struck; "two slits" is 4490's idealization.) Averaged over photon directions, the fringe contrast is the average of that phase factor. The script's
table, verbatim (D-11):

Three things follow, each observed:

- **The blur depends on how far apart the arms are, and partly revives (K1).** With the paths close together a bounce does almost nothing; at about half a
  wavelength apart the fringes vanish; farther apart they partly return. Chapman et al. saw exactly this: contrast loss and revivals as the separation of the
  interfering paths at the point of scattering was increased. **The condition on his picture:** the kick must reach the guiding arcs in both arms,
  as one shared kick. A kick felt only by the arcs on the side the atom's core took could not make the loss depend on the arms' separation, and could not make
  it revive. In his words, the photons collide "with one or the other side"; read at its strongest, the photon meets the atom's whole guiding volume, both
  sides together. (Claude's statement of the condition; §5 asks him.)
- **Faint bounces add up (K2).** With light ten times longer in wavelength than the arm separation, one bounce leaves 92% of the contrast; ten leave 45%;
  a hundred leave almost none. This is his answer in numbers, and it is what Kokorowski et al. (2001; 4490 §2.2 (3)) observed.
- **Sorting brings the fringes back (K3).** At the separation where all atoms together show no fringes, the atoms whose photon went into a narrow cone show
  sharp fringes again, shifted. Chapman et al. did this: they recovered the lost coherence by observing only atoms correlated with photons emitted into a
  limited angular range. **His pointillism explains it** (Claude's reading): each atom lands by its own kicked pattern, so the screen holds many sharp
  patterns, each shifted by its own kick; the blur is their sum, and picking the atoms with the same kick picks one sharp pattern back out. For that to
  work, the kick must be written in the photon as well as in the atom — his "will entangle with the Photon DPs."

**Energy.** "That collision probably takes a little energy from the atom." In this experiment the atom absorbs the photon and re-emits it, returning to its
ground state; its energy changes by a tiny recoil amount, up or down (on average slightly up, which is recoil heating). So "a little" is right in size; what
matters is the momentum.

**Kick and record (D-10, 4480).** In this experiment the kick and the photon's record of the path are one effect described two ways (4480, with Pfau et al.
1994 and this same Chapman et al. 1995). Where the path is marked with no kick — in polarization, as in the Walborn eraser (§4); and Dürr, Nonn and Rempe
(Nature 395, 33, 1998), where the back-action was too small to explain the disappearance — the fringes still go. So the kick account covers scattering, but
the general cause is the record. His picture needs, beside the kick, an account of how a record with no kick changes where the guiding arcs send the atom.

## 4. The Walborn eraser, in plain terms

He was not sure what the 4490 correction meant. In Walborn et al. (2002; filed at 4445 and 4481 §2), a twin pair of photons is made. One twin, A, goes
through two slits. Just before each slit sits a plate that gives A's polarization a twist: left-handed at one slit, right-handed at the other. So **which
slit A took is written into A's own polarization**, like a tag on A itself. A tagged photon makes no fringes: the pattern on the screen is smooth.

The twin, B, never goes near the slits. But B's polarization is linked to A's (that is their entanglement). If B is sent through a polarizer and A's hits are
counted only together with B's result — the polarizer set first at one angle, then, in a separate run, at the perpendicular angle — each run shows fringes,
one set and then its opposite, because within each run the tag on A can no longer be read. Added together, the two sets fill each other's gaps and the
screen is smooth again. It is the case of §3.2's last paragraph: the path is marked, the fringes go, and nothing kicked A.

What 4490 corrected: an earlier draft said the which-slit record was held by the partner, B. It is held by A's own polarization; B's role is to let the
experimenter sort A's hits so that the tag is erased within each group.

## 7. Reply to the founder

Agreed on distance and collision. A tie isn't worn down by distance. It's spread out when one partner hits other things, and "dispersal" is the right word,
since the entanglement is passed on, not destroyed.

On the faint bounces, your answer is that each one counts: every bounce kicks the atom, every kick changes the arcs a little, and the blurs add up. That's
your 4464 picture again, with the arcs through both slits guiding the atom, and it is consistent with the standard account under conditions. The kick has
to reach the arcs in both slits. The experiment (Chapman and Pritchard's group, 1995) shows why: the blur depends on how far apart the two paths are where
the photon hits, and past about half a wavelength apart the fringes partly come back. A kick felt only on one side couldn't do that. They also got the
fringes back by keeping only the atoms whose photon went in one direction, which is your pointillism: each atom lands by its own kicked pattern, and
sorting by kick picks out one sharp pattern.

One caution: for scattering, the kick and the record of which way the atom went are the same thing. But a path can also be marked with no kick at all, and
the fringes still go. So the general cause is the record, and your picture needs that case too.

The Walborn experiment, plainly: just before each slit a plate gives the photon's polarization a twist, left-handed at one slit and right-handed at the
other, so the slit it took is written on the photon itself, and it makes no fringes. Its twin's polarization is linked to it. If the photon's hits are counted
only together with the twin's result at a polarizer, set at one angle and then at the perpendicular angle, each run shows fringes. Nothing kicked the photon;
the tag alone did it.
