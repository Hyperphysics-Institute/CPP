## 2. The solid datum: agreed, with a qualifier

The same rule ties wavelength to momentum for light and for matter: λ = h ÷ p. For a photon, p = E ÷ c, so λ = hc ÷ E, which is his "wavelength corresponds to
its energy content"; for a particle, λ = h ÷ p, de Broglie's. Electrons build up fringes one dot at a time (Tonomura 1989; 4443, 4444); neutrons and atoms
interfere at their de Broglie wavelength (4444); and so do large molecules (C₆₀, Arndt and others, 1999; molecules above 25,000 atomic mass units, Fein and
others, 2019). (For the neutral beams, "one at a time" means beams dilute enough that each particle interferes only with itself.)

**One difference matters for his unifying cause:** for a particle it is the momentum, not the total energy content, that sets the wavelength; a particle at
rest carries its whole energy but has no finite wavelength. For a photon the two coincide (E = pc). So in CPP it must be the part of the DP-arc cohort that goes
with motion, not the whole cohort, that sets the crest spacing (= 4443 c, PROP-6).

So the common rule is measured, and in CPP his common carrier, the DP-arc-separation cohort, is the postulate to be made causal. His task, in Claude's words:
to derive λ = h ÷ p, and with it h, from the DP arcs (= 4464 d for particles, 4470 b for photons). A fact for CPP to match, not evidence for it.

## 3. Identical wavelets: what they explain, and what they need (script)

**What identical drops explain.** If the central CP and each polarized DP drop through the same energy difference, and if the rate is set by that difference
(owed: 4470 a, b), each wavelet has the same rate, so all of them share the same centre colour (though each short wavelet on its own is still smeared). In
standard physics that is why every atom of one kind gives the same line: the same energy difference. (Each wavelet would then carry a share of the energy, not
its own E = hf; the colour comes from the shared rate, not from each share. Many wavelets in step and without gaps are one extended excitation, absorbed whole
(4470 §2), so this does not revive "many waves each carrying part of the energy" as many quanta.)

**What makes the line narrow is how the wavelets are arranged in time.** In the script, fifty identical wavelets, each three cycles long:

| Arrangement | Spread of colour (share of the frequency) |
|---|---|
| one wavelet | 0.29 |
| fifty at the same moment, side by side | 0.29 (one bigger short pulse) |
| fifty at random moments, independent | about 0.29 (the single-wavelet spread, within noise) |
| fifty one after another, in step, no gaps | 0.006 (a long train; 0.9 ÷ 150) |

- **Side by side**, the wavelets add into one bigger pulse of the same short length: the same spread of colour. More contributions across the photon's width
  do not sharpen it (4470 §2, Copilot's point).
- **At random moments**, each keeps its own broad spread, and the sum stays broad (4468 §3).
- **One after another, in step, and with no gaps** (each starting no later than the previous one ends; in the script, exactly one wavelet-length, three cycles,
  later), they join into one long wave of fifty times three crests, and the line narrows to about 0.9 ÷ (number of crests). If gaps are left between in-step

  at 0.8, 1.0 and 1.2 of the frequency; 4468 §3). If their starts wander, power leaks out of the narrow line into a broad base: at a tenth of a cycle about
  seven-tenths of the light stays in the narrow line; at a quarter-cycle wander, only about a fifth.

**So identical wavelets give a single fine line only if they come in sequence, in step, without gaps: many wavelets in step and without gaps are one long
wave.** That is Copilot's model A, a long phase-locked train (4470 §4), built from his identical drops. It also answers his "needing multiple waves": for
sharpness the grating needs, in each photon, one wave with many crests; many photons build up the line's brightness, click by click (4468 §2, 4469 §2), not its

## 7. Answer to his "What do you think?"

His solid datum is right, and it's a good place to stand. The same rule ties wavelength to momentum for light and for matter, and electrons, neutrons, atoms
and even large molecules make fringes at that wavelength. One qualifier: for matter it's the momentum that sets the wavelength, not the total energy; a
particle sitting still has all its energy but no wavelength. So in CPP it should be the moving part of the DP-arc cohort that sets the spacing. The task he
names, showing how the cohort produces that rule, is the right one.

Identical drops help with one thing: if the central CP and every polarized DP drop through the same energy difference, and the rate follows that difference,
they all share the same centre colour (though each short wavelet on its own is still smeared). That's also why every atom of one kind gives the same line. But
identical wavelets only make a single fine line if they come one after another, in step and with no gaps between them. Side by side, fifty identical short
wavelets just add up to one bigger short pulse, with the same smeared colour; at random moments they stay smeared; with gaps between them they split into
several lines. One after another, in step and without gaps, they join into one long wave, and the line becomes sharp. So many wavelets in step are one long
wave: for sharpness the grating needs many crests in each photon, while many photons only make the line brighter.

That means if the drop happened all at once, the line would be broad; a fine line needs the drop to give up its energy a wavelet at a time, in step and
without gaps, over many cycles. So I've asked: when the central CP and its polarized DPs each make the same drop, do they drop all at once, one after another,
or something else, and if one after another, what keeps each in step with the last, with no gap?
