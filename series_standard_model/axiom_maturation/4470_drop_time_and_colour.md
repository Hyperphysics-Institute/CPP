## 3. Does the drop time set the colour? Three readings (script)

He wrote that the drop time "corresponds to" the frequency, with a monotone mechanism: a faster drop makes a faster cohort, more polarization, a higher
frequency. Three readings of "corresponds to" are tested here.

**First, the trend he points to is real.** In standard physics decay rates go roughly as frequency cubed times the square of the atom's dipole, so across
lines, higher-frequency lines tend to decay faster: Lyman-α is fast and high, the 21 cm line is extremely slow and low. That is consistent with his "faster
drop, higher frequency". The question is whether the drop's time *sets* the colour.

**Reading 1: the drop lasts one period** (drop time = 1 ÷ frequency). Then the drop is one cycle, and one cycle's colour is spread as wide as itself (4464 §3,
4467 §3). That fails the line width for an ordinary wave of one cycle. A one-cycle photon with a narrow line would need Copilot's model B, a short object whose
own response is narrow; it would have to behave unlike measured single photons (4469 §4), and that is the open question 4468(a) and 4469 §6.

**Reading 2: the drop lasts the measured decay time.** That time does not set the colour (script):

| Line | Colour (frequency) | Upper-level decay time | 1 ÷ decay time | Misses by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lyman-α, 2p → 1s | 2.47 × 10¹⁵ Hz (ultraviolet) | 1.6 ns | 6 × 10⁸ Hz (radio) | 4 × 10⁶ |
| Balmer-α, 3p → 2s | 4.57 × 10¹⁴ Hz (red) | 5.3 ns | 2 × 10⁸ Hz | 2 × 10⁶ |
| Balmer-α, 3d → 2p | nearly the same red | 15.6 ns | 6 × 10⁷ Hz | 7 × 10⁶ |
| Balmer-α, 3s → 2p | nearly the same red | 158 ns | 6 × 10⁶ Hz | 7 × 10⁷ |
| 21 cm, hydrogen's hyperfine line | 1.42 × 10⁹ Hz (radio) | about 11 million years | 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ Hz | 5 × 10²³ |

The same red line, the same to about 2 parts in 10⁵ (fine structure), comes from three levels whose decay times are thirtyfold apart, and 1 ÷ decay time misses
the colour by a factor of 10⁶ (optical) to 10²⁴ (21 cm). The 21 cm line shows that the colour is not 1 ÷ the time; it is not evidence against his monotone
link, which it fits. (The decay times are level lifetimes from click-time histograms; 3p decays mostly, about 88%, by Lyman-β, and about 12% by Balmer-α; 4466
§2.)

**Reading 3: the drop lasts a fixed number of periods** (N ÷ frequency, N large). Then the drop time would track the colour by construction, and the line could
be narrow. But it would track it only if N were fixed: then every photon of one colour would have the same length and the same spread of colour. The three
Balmer-α routes have nearly the same colour but natural widths that follow the levels' lifetimes, not the colour (computed from both levels' decay rates:
about 3 × 10⁷ Hz from 3p, about 1 × 10⁸ Hz from 3s and 3d; script). And photon lengths measured on single emitters follow the emitter's lifetime (4466 §3). So
N is not fixed, and the colour is not set by the drop's length. (Whether measured Balmer-α component widths confirm the computed ones is owed, (g).)

**So the drop time does not set the colour** under any of the three readings, though faster drops do go with higher colours across lines.

## 4. The split that fits (Claude's reading, marked)

His two ideas fit together if they play different roles:
- **The colour is the eigenmode's rate.** The emitting cohort (central CP and polarized DP cloud) rings at a collective rate. His "faster shell drop is a
  higher-velocity cohort, hence more DP-arc polarization" is read as: a larger energy difference makes a faster mode ("stiffer" is Claude's word, not his). The
  rate must come out at ΔE/h (4461 a).
- **How long it rings sets the width, together with the lower level.** The drop's duration sets the number of cycles in the photon, and so contributes to the
  line's narrowness: ringing for N cycles gives a spread of about 1 ÷ N (4467 §3). In standard physics the natural width comes from the lifetimes of both the
  upper and lower levels (for Balmer-α from 3s and 3d, mostly the lower level, 2p). The cycle counts (frequency × upper lifetime) are millions for the optical
  lines and, formally, some 10²³ for the 21 cm line (in space its observed width is set by collisions and motion); they describe the upper level's emission
  envelope only.

Here the eigenmode belongs to the emitting cohort, and its ringing lays down a long train (Copilot's model A). Copilot's model B, a short photon whose own
eigenmode is narrow, is a different picture; whether CPP allows it is the open question 4468(a) and 4469 §6. Which cohort's eigenmode he means is not settled,
and the reassignment is Claude's (marked).

This split matches standard physics in outline: the rate from the energy difference; the width from the lifetimes of both levels. It changes two things he
said: the drop time sets the narrowness, not the colour; and his velocity-and-polarization link becomes a stiffness of the mode (Claude's word, not his). For a
CPP derivation, Copilot's list stands: the cohort's dynamical variable, the equation it obeys, the eigenfrequency from it (= ΔE/h), the ringing time, the
grating coupling and the line shape.

## 7. Answer to his "What do you think?"

Copilot's analysis is good, and I agree with most of it. Its way of explaining the grating is clearer than how I led: a short wave has a wide spread of
colours, and the grating just spreads those colours out; the narrowness belongs to the light. It also makes a point that matters for his new aspect: many
contributions side by side, like his central CP plus its polarized DP cloud, make the photon wide, but they don't add cycles along its travel, and it's the
cycles that make the line sharp. And I'd describe the photon as one extended excitation absorbed whole, rather than many waves each carrying part of the
energy, since it always arrives as one click carrying all of it.

On his postulate, the eigenmode half can work, if CPP can show it. Across different lines, faster drops do tend to go with higher colours, as he says. But a
drop's time isn't what fixes the colour: the same red, to within a few parts in a hundred thousand, comes from drops thirtyfold apart. Read as a period, the
measured decay times miss the colour by a factor of a million or more: Lyman-α's 1.6 nanoseconds would make it a radio wave, and hydrogen's 21 cm line, which
takes about 11 million years per atom, still rings 1.4 billion times a second. And if a drop always lasted the same number of cycles, every photon of one
colour would have the same sharpness, which those red-line routes don't.

What fits is to give the two ideas different jobs. The cohort rings at a rate set by the energy difference, and that rate is the colour. How long it rings
helps set how sharp the line is (the lower level's lifetime counts too). That's my reading of his idea, and it changes two things he said, so I've asked
whether that's how he sees it, or whether he sees the drop's speed setting the colour, and if so how three drops of very different lengths give the same red.
